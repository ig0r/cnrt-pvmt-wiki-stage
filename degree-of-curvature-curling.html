    Degree of Curvature

    curling-indicatorcurvaturepavement-measurement
    Also known as: slab curvature rate
    Created 2026-07-22 · Updated 2026-08-16T12:25:16Z

    The degree of curvature quantifies the rate at which a surface’s slope changes along its length, serving as a fundamental geometric descriptor for structural deformation. Mathematically, it is defined as the second derivative of an elevation profile normalized by the magnitude of the first derivative, linking directly to the radius of curvature where higher values correspond to tighter bends [1]. For surfaces with gentle slopes, this measure simplifies to the second derivative of elevation with respect to distance, providing a practical approximation for engineering analysis [1]. This metric is widely employed in structural and pavement engineering to assess how environmental gradients induce deformation in continuous slabs [1].

    Impact of Curling and Warping on Concrete Pavement: Phase II (TR-749) (2023)

    Key findings

    • Temperature differentials fundamentally drive slab curvature, with shrinkage-based equivalent temperature differences exerting a greater impact than hourly or overall temperature components [1].
    • Degree of curvature exhibits seasonal variation, peaking in the fall and reaching its minimum in spring [1].
    • Wider slabs and thinner sections exhibit higher curvature compared to narrower or thicker configurations [1].
    • Larger dowel bars, rectangular joints, tied PCC shoulders, and newer QMC mixes consistently lower the degree of curvature [1].
    • High-strength concrete mixes raise the degree of curvature relative to other mix designs [1].

    Degree of Curvature Analysis and Definition

    The degree of curvature quantifies the rate at which a surface’s slope changes along its length, providing a mathematical measure of how sharply an elevation profile bends [1]. It is derived from the second derivative of the elevation function relative to distance, often simplified for gentle slopes where the effect of the surface gradient on the calculation becomes negligible [1]. This metric establishes an inverse relationship with the radius of curvature and serves as a fundamental parameter in structural analysis for evaluating deformation caused by environmental gradients [1].

    Research problem — There is a lack of a consistent, unit-based method for quantifying the degree of curvature caused by curling and warping in jointed plain concrete pavements [1]. Engineers currently lack a standardized quantitative technique to relate environmental gradients or design choices to slab deformation and compare severity across projects [1].

    State of the art — The current state of practice for quantifying concrete pavement curling relies on extracting the degree of curvature from high-resolution surface profiles using algorithms such as the Second-Generation Curvature Index and second-order polynomial fits, often augmented by stationary LiDAR scanners to generate full three-dimensional point clouds [1].

    Findings from the reports

    • Confirmed that an algorithm can reliably extract the degree of curvature from high-speed inertial profiler data, with field validation showing agreement to walking profilers and independent portable curling/warping devices [1].
    • Observed that shrinkage-equivalent temperature differences have a greater impact on curvature than hourly or overall temperature components [1].
    • Measured systematic seasonal variations in degree of curvature, with fall exhibiting the highest average values and spring showing the lowest across the network [1].
    • Found that diurnal patterns influence curvature, with measurements peaking in the early morning and reaching their lowest levels at noon [1].
    • Reported that larger dowel bars and tied PCC shoulders consistently reduce the degree of curvature compared to smaller dowels or untied shoulder configurations [1].
    • Showed that joint spacing affects curvature, with narrower transverse spacing yielding higher curvature values than wider spacing intervals [1].
    • Identified that mix design impacts curvature levels, with newer QMC mixes lowering curvature while high-strength mixes tend to increase it [1].

    Novelty & rigor — The research introduces a novel, fully automated methodology that extracts the degree of curvature directly from raw high-speed inertial profiler data by integrating joint detection with a dual-model fitting approach comprising a Second-Generation Curvature Index and a second-order polynomial fit [1]. This distinct pipeline isolates true slab curling and warping from overall grade to generate an objective numeric metric, alongside complementary indicators such as curvature IRI and deflection ratio, thereby providing a repeatable quantitative assessment previously unavailable in standard profiling workflows [1]. The approach ensures high reproducibility through a tightly controlled workflow that specifies exact equipment calibration, seasonal sampling schedules, and the use of provided MATLAB scripts to process elevation profiles into consistent curvature values validated against independent field measurements [1].

    Implications — Design and maintenance strategies should prioritize the degree of curvature as the primary metric for evaluating slab curling severity [1]. Specifiers ought to select wider and thicker slab sections, along with larger dowels and specific joint configurations, to mitigate higher curvature in narrower or thinner geometries [1]. Construction practices should target cooler seasonal windows for paving and employ mix designs that reduce moisture gradients to minimize curling magnitude [1].

    Limitations — The reliability of the climate data used is uncertain, and the weak correlation with temperature differences suggests that this variable may not be a primary driver [1]. Statistical models have limited explanatory power due to low fit metrics and the exclusion of predictors required to meet normality assumptions [1]. Measurement accuracy is compromised by ambiguous visual checks on flat surfaces and errors in joint detection caused by distress or irregular travel paths [1].
