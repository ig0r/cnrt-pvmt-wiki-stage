    Eddy Current Testing for Pavements

    eddy-currentnondestructive-testingpavement-thicknessquality-assurance
    Also known as: Eddy Current Testing for Concrete, Electromagnetic Thickness Measurement
    Created 2026-05-20 · Updated 2026-08-17T17:32:10Z

    Eddy current testing for pavements is a nondestructive electromagnetic technique that utilizes an alternating-current coil to generate a magnetic field for assessing subsurface conditions. When this magnetic field encounters a conductive element, such as a metal plate embedded beneath the pavement, it induces eddy currents within that conductor [1]. Because concrete is non-conductive and does not generate eddy currents, the sensor primarily measures the distance to the hidden conductive reference rather than the material itself [1]. By correlating changes in the coil’s impedance with the separation between the sensor and the conductor, the method estimates pavement thickness or detects the presence of underlying metallic components [1].

    Eddy Current Thickness Measurement

    Eddy current testing is a nondestructive electromagnetic technique that employs an alternating-current coil to generate a magnetic field for assessing material properties [1]. When this field interacts with a conductive element, it induces eddy currents that alter the coil’s impedance, whereas non-conductive materials like concrete do not produce such effects [1]. By correlating changes in the coil’s impedance components to the distance between the sensor and a hidden conductive reference, the method can estimate the thickness of overlying pavement layers [1].

    Research problem — The research addresses the need to replace costly and labor-intensive concrete core sampling with nondestructive eddy current methods for verifying pavement thickness, as small measurement errors significantly impact contractor payments [1]. A key challenge is that standard eddy current techniques cannot directly detect non-conductive concrete, requiring the development of practical solutions involving conductive target plates and careful management of placement variables relative to reinforcement [1].

    State of the art — Eddy current testing for pavement thickness remains in an experimental phase, as the technique is currently limited to detecting conductive plates beneath concrete rather than sensing the concrete itself [1]. While proof-of-concept tests confirm that impedance changes correlate with distance to a conductive target, practical deployment requires further validation of accuracy and calibration protocols before it can replace core sampling [1].

    Findings from the reports

    • Demonstrated that wet or dry concrete is essentially transparent to the magnetic field, allowing an eddy-current coil to reliably detect buried metal plates [1].
    • Showed that the sensor’s real and imaginary impedance varies noticeably as the depth of a conductive target changes until it approaches roughly the coil diameter [1].
    • Confirmed that practical pavement-thickness monitoring requires embedding conductive plates (such as in rebar cages) beneath the slab and using a larger-diameter coil matched to the expected concrete thickness [1].
    • Established that scaling the coil diameter to match typical slab thickness makes the method applicable for real-world pavement applications [1].
    • Identified that practical implementation can take the form of handheld units or paver-mounted sensor banks, enabling on-the-fly readings while concrete is still wet [1].
    • Revealed that compared with laser scanning, the electromagnetic approach yields discrete point data rather than continuous profiles, making laser scanning preferred for high-accuracy, real-time thickness profiling [1].

    Novelty & rigor — The research introduces a novel approach that treats the concrete slab as a transparent medium by embedding conductive plates beneath the pavement, allowing eddy-current sensors to infer thickness directly from impedance changes without destructive sampling [1]. This method enables continuous, real-time monitoring of pavement thickness and dowel-bar depth during construction by mounting sensor arrays on paving equipment or using handheld units for quality control [1]. Reproducibility is achieved through the use of standardized test cells with known conductive plates, specific coil dimensions, and daily calibration against manual measurements to ensure accurate impedance readings across varying concrete states [1].

    Implications — Eddy current sensing offers a practical, near-term alternative to laser scanning for verifying pavement thickness and dowel-bar placement by enabling real-time data collection without labor-intensive coring [1]. Because the technique provides discrete rather than continuous coverage, agencies must develop and calibrate dedicated handheld or paver-mounted devices before widespread adoption to ensure accuracy across typical pavement depths [1].

    Limitations — The method provides only discrete point measurements rather than continuous coverage, making it less suitable than laser scanning for comprehensive monitoring [1]. Field implementation is constrained by the need to place a conductive plate beneath the slab and position sensors away from reinforcing steel to avoid interference [1]. The technique requires frequent calibration, potentially on a daily basis, and remains secondary until handheld or paver-mounted devices are developed [1].
