    Spectral Analysis of Surface Waves (SASW)

    ndtnondestructive-testingpavement-evaluationstress-wave
    Also known as: SASW
    Created 2026-05-20 · Updated 2026-08-17T10:33:44Z

    Spectral Analysis of Surface Waves (SASW) is a nondestructive testing method that characterizes the subsurface mechanical properties of materials such as pavements and soils. The technique operates by generating Rayleigh-type surface waves using an impulsive source and recording their propagation with spaced receivers to capture frequency-dependent phase velocity data [1] [2]. These recorded signals are transformed into the frequency domain to derive dispersion curves that describe how wave speed varies with wavelength [1] [2]. By inverting this dispersion data, depth-dependent shear wave velocities and associated elastic constants like shear modulus can be determined without requiring prior knowledge of layer thicknesses [1] [2].

    SASW Elastic Property Profiling

    SASW is a nondestructive testing method that generates Rayleigh-type surface waves using an impact source and records the resulting motion with spaced receivers [1] [2]. The technique extracts frequency-dependent phase velocity from these signals to construct dispersion curves that describe how wave speed varies with wavelength [1] [2]. By inverting this dispersion data, the method determines depth-dependent shear wave velocities and derives elastic constants such as shear modulus without requiring prior knowledge of layer thicknesses [1] [2].

    Research problem — The practical deployment of SASW for elastic property profiling is hindered by the requirement for highly skilled operators and extensive signal-processing expertise, which limits its mobility and user-friendliness on active roadways [1]. Evaluating rigid pavements presents a significant challenge in obtaining reliable elastic-property data when access is restricted to one side and the thicknesses of constituent layers are unknown, necessitating methods that can generate stiffness profiles without prior knowledge of layer geometry [2].

    State of the art — SASW is an established seismic-based nondestructive evaluation technique used to determine pavement and soil elastic properties, particularly for thin concrete slabs where it generates stiffness profiles without requiring prior knowledge of layer thickness [2]. The method relies on transforming recorded waveforms into phase-velocity dispersion curves via FFT and inverting them to derive shear-wave velocity, shear modulus, and dynamic Young’s modulus with depth [1] [2]. Although SASW is integrated into multi-technique platforms like the Seismic Pavement Analyzer to assess subgrade conditions and detect voids, its routine field deployment remains limited by the need for skilled operators and sophisticated signal-processing software [1] [2].

    Findings from the reports

    • Demonstrated that Spectral Analysis of Surface Waves (SASW) can determine the elastic-property profile of a pavement, including layer stiffness and the depth of deteriorated concrete [1].
    • Identified that SASW requires an experienced operator and complex signal-processing for accurate results [1].

    Novelty & rigor — The research does not introduce novel SASW techniques, instead treating it as an established method for determining stiffness profiles that relies on complex signal processing and expert interpretation [1]. Reproducibility depends on a conventional setup involving impact sources and multiple receivers to derive dispersion curves, which are then inverted using standard equations to calculate elastic constants without reference to specific ASTM standards [2].

    Implications — Practitioners should reserve SASW for targeted condition assessments of pavement systems rather than rapid, in-service surveys due to its inability to operate at highway speeds [1]. Successful deployment requires allocating resources for skilled operators and sophisticated signal-processing capabilities to accurately map stiffness profiles and locate deteriorated concrete layers [1].

    Limitations — The method requires sophisticated signal processing and expert interpretation, which reduces its practicality for routine field applications [1]. Because transducers must maintain physical contact with the pavement surface, traffic control is necessary, preventing high-speed surveys of in-service roads [1].
