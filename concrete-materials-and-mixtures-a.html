    Concrete Materials & Mixtures

    aggregateconcrete-materialsfiber-reinforced-concretehydrationmix-designworkability
    Also known as: Cementitious Materials, Concrete Mix Design and Rheology, Fiber-Reinforced Concrete Mixtures, Supplementary Cementitious Materials (SCM)
    Created 2026-07-28 · Updated 2026-08-13

    Concrete materials and mixtures constitute a composite construction system primarily composed of cementitious binders, aggregates, water, and chemical admixtures that interact to form a durable structural material. The mechanical strength and durability of the hardened matrix are fundamentally governed by the hydration process, an exothermic chemical reaction where cementitious materials react with water to form binding phases that dictate long-term performance [26] [29] [34] [38]. Supplementary cementitious materials function as finely divided substances that supplement portland cement paste to enhance properties such as long-term strength and chemical resistance through pozzolanic reactions or inherent hydration [15] [34]. Fiber-reinforced concrete incorporates discrete fibers distributed throughout the matrix to provide tensile reinforcement via crack bridging, thereby mitigating brittleness and enhancing post-crack ductility [33] [40]. Fresh concrete workability and mixing ensure the uniform distribution of constituents to achieve desired consistency, while specialized mixtures such as self-consolidating or roller-compacted concrete employ specific rheological balances and compaction methods to meet distinct placement requirements [5] [17] [19].

    Key findings

    • Internal curing using lightweight fine aggregate or superabsorbent polymers mitigated autogenous and plastic shrinkage cracking while enhancing the degree of hydration, improving long-term durability and extending predicted service life [3] [12] [14].
    • Ternary mixtures utilizing supplementary cementitious materials such as fly ash, slag, silica fume, and metakaolin demonstrated excellent long-term compressive strength potential and durability, with performance heavily influenced by bulk chemistry and allowing for significant reductions in portland cement content [29] [39].
    • Construction-related issues such as retempering and elevated water-cementitious ratios exerted a greater negative influence on deicer scaling performance than the inclusion of slag cement or other supplementary materials in the mixture [16] [31].
    • Isothermal calorimetry effectively differentiated heat evolution among mortars with varying cement types and supplementary materials, enabling the prediction of set time, early-age strength, and material incompatibilities for quality control [34] [36].
    • Two-stage mixing produced concrete with superior homogeneity, increased tensile strength, and greater resistance to chloride penetration compared to conventional ready-mixed methods, yielding higher compressive strengths despite challenges with air entrainment [5].
    • Reducing the water-to-cementitious ratio significantly decreased deicer scaling susceptibility, with lower ratios allowing mixtures to pass mass loss requirements regardless of slag content or test method [30].
    • Optimizing binder content involved balancing the critical paste volume needed for consolidation against durability penalties, as increasing cementitious content beyond the threshold for workability directly increased chloride penetrability and air permeability [38].

    Concrete Mix Proportioning And Durability

    Findings from the reports

    • Air entrainment enhanced pervious concrete by increasing paste volume and workability, which reduced porosity and increased unit weight, thereby improving compressive strength significantly [8] [9].
    • Low-permeability concrete made with Type IP cement achieved exceptional freezing-thawing durability without air entrainment due to high compressive strength and low porosity [22].
    • Increasing cementitious content beyond the critical paste volume threshold yielded diminishing returns on compressive strength but significantly increased chloride penetrability and air permeability [38].
    • Mixtures containing fly ash showed better workability than those with slag or limestone powder due to lower specific surface areas adsorbing less mixing water in pervious concrete applications [9].
    • Pollutant abatement tests revealed that mixes containing fly ash and limestone powder removed approximately 30% of naphthalene concentration, whereas pure cement and slag mixes performed poorly [9].

    Calorimetry And Hydration Monitoring

    Findings from the reports

    • Demonstrated that simple isothermal calorimetry on sieved mortar samples provides consistent heat evolution signatures suitable for daily quality control and detecting material incompatibility [34] [36].
    • Developed heat indexes derived from calorimetry curves to predict early-age strength and detect material incompatibilities such as flash set caused by specific cement-fly ash combinations [34] [36].
    • Integrated calorimetry results with the HIPERPAV computer program to provide a reliable mechanism for estimating thermal cracking risk and simulating in-situ pavement temperatures [34] [36].
    • Confirmed that early-age concrete strength correlates linearly with generated heat, allowing for the prediction of saw-cutting times and pavement performance [34].
    • Showed that variations in water reducer or fly ash dosages shift heat generation curves without necessarily indicating incompatibility, enabling the establishment of acceptable heat evolution boundaries for field evaluation [36].

    Evidence: 2 reports

    Deicer Scaling Resistance Factors

    Findings from the reports

    • Construction-related issues, such as retempering and elevated water-cementitious material ratios, exerted a greater influence on deicer scaling performance than the inclusion of slag cement or specific mixture proportions [16] [31].
    • Air-void spacing factors served as a reliable indicator of freeze-thaw resistance, with values of 0.008 inches or less typically correlating with good performance [16] [31].
    • Reducing the water-to-cementitious material ratio significantly improved both deicer scaling resistance and compressive strength, allowing tested mixes to pass mass loss requirements regardless of slag content [30].
    • The use of low-alkali cement reduced deicer scaling susceptibility compared to high-alkali alternatives, particularly when slag replacement levels were increased [30].
    • A drying period following accelerated curing significantly reduced deicer scaling by preventing excessive surface saturation prior to freeze-thaw cycling [30].
    • Standard laboratory test methods often indicated severe scaling for high-slag mixtures, whereas field surveys demonstrated that these concretes performed well in actual service environments [31].

    Evidence: 3 reports

    Internal Curing Agents

    Findings from the reports

    • Lightweight fine aggregate (LWFA) used for internal curing improved permeability characteristics, evidenced by lower rapid chloride permeability values and higher surface resistivity, while maintaining mechanical properties comparable to control mixes [13] [14].
    • Internal curing formulations using LWFA or superabsorbent polymers mitigated autogenous and plastic shrinkage cracking by supplying internal water that did not contribute to the effective water-cementitious material ratio [3] [12].
    • Internal curing with LWFA enhanced the degree of hydration and increased compressive strength at later ages (90 days) compared to conventional concrete, while buffering moisture gradients to significantly reduce slab warping and curling [13].
    • Superabsorbent polymers (SAPs) enhanced workability retention by reducing slump loss and created a more stable air void system compared to traditional chemical air-entraining agents [12].
    • Incorporating SAPs into concrete decreased compressive strength by approximately 8% at 28 and 56 days and lowered electrical resistivity due to residual porosity left by desorbed polymer particles [12].
    • Adding dry SAP was less detrimental to overall concrete performance than using presoaked SAP, although both methods resulted in reduced strength and resistivity compared to control mixes [12].

    Evidence: 4 reports

    Mix Design for Shrinkage Control

    Findings from the reports

    • Coarse aggregates with lower water absorption and fine aggregates with coarser gradations reduced warping tendencies in the concrete mixtures [37].
    • Maintaining moderate water-cementitious ratios prevented the exacerbation of either drying or autogenous shrinkage that resulted from deviations to higher or lower extremes [37].
    • Replacing a portion of portland cement with Class F fly ash improved resistance to plastic shrinkage and enhanced workability and long-term durability [40].
    • Type K shrinkage-compensating cement increased the rate of capillary pressure development, which negatively affected the concrete’s resistance to plastic shrinkage [40].
    • Polypropylene microfibers at dosages up to 1.0% by volume enhanced tensile strength and reduced cracking potential, though practical limits were set at 0.125% due to increased water demand [40].
    • Alkali-resistant glass macrofibers demonstrated superior synergy with polypropylene microfibers, providing better compressive strength and post-peak residual toughness compared to other fiber types [40].

    Evidence: 2 reports

    3D Printable Concrete Mixtures

    Findings from the reports

    • Observed that silica fume and viscosity-modifying agents decreased flowability and extrudability but increased buildability, whereas superplasticizers improved flowability at the expense of stability [6].
    • Demonstrated that printed samples exhibited lower compressive strength than mold-cast counterparts due to weak interlayer bonds, yet showed superior flexural strength attributed to densification during extrusion [6].
    • Revealed significant anisotropic behavior in the printed concrete, with compressive strength being much lower when loaded parallel to the filaments compared to perpendicular loading [6].

    Evidence: 1 report

    Air-Void Analyzer Testing

    Findings from the reports

    • Observed that Air-Void Analyzer (AVA) tests exhibited higher variation than Modified Point Count (C457) methods and showed weak relationships with total air content measured by ASTM C231 [7].
    • Established that ASTM C231 yielded higher air content values than AVA, with little to no relationship observed between the total air content values measured by these two methods [7].
    • Revealed a strong correlation (R² = 0.82) between the AVA spacing factor and the content of small air voids (≤ 300μm) [7].
    • Determined that ambient temperature and water-cementitious material ratios notably affected AVA results, whereas paver vibration primarily impacted gravimetric measurements rather than small air voids detected by the AVA [7].
    • Found that implementing a properly specified test procedure could significantly reduce the variation of AVA measurements in concrete practice [7].

    Evidence: 1 report

    Electrically Conductive Concrete Production

    Findings from the reports

    • The study demonstrated that adding carbon fibers in two stages at the job site prevented clumping and ensured even distribution within the concrete [11].
    • The research established that limiting batch sizes to 6 cubic yards per truck further reduced fiber balling during production [11].
    • The investigation revealed that fresh-stage electrical resistance testing on each batch before acceptance onsite was required to ensure the mix met target conductivity specifications [11].

    Evidence: 1 report

    Hydration Monitoring And Saw-Cutting Timing

    Findings from the reports

    • Evaluated concrete mixtures containing various aggregates and supplementary cementitious materials to correlate initial set times with saw-cutting windows, establishing that ultrasonic pulse velocity (UPV) provides a reliable method for predicting the onset of the sawing window [28].
    • Revealed that calorimetric data confirmed hydration kinetics were influenced by mixture chemistry and temperature, but the thermal fraction method demonstrated poor predictive correlation for setting times compared to UPV [28].

    Evidence: 1 report

    Iowa Concrete Material Properties

    Findings from the reports

    • Artificial neural network models achieved a higher correlation coefficient than nonlinear regression when predicting compressive strength using water-to-binder ratio, unit weight, cementitious material factor, and age [32].
    • Preliminary drying shrinkage testing indicated that the ultimate shrinkage of typical Iowa concrete closely aligned with standard MEPDG default parameters in both magnitude and development time [32].

    Evidence: 1 report

    Material Quality and Mix Proportioning

    Findings from the reports

    • Established the requirement for proper air entrainment systems to ensure durability against environmental conditions [41].

    Evidence: 1 report

    Portable XRF Concrete Analysis

    Findings from the reports

    • Evaluated a portable X-ray fluorescence device for assessing concrete mix proportions, finding it provided reasonable analyses of raw cementitious materials and accurately predicted supplementary cementitious material dosages in paste mixtures when ignoring water content [24].
    • Observed that the portable X-ray fluorescence device struggled with heterogeneous mortar samples due to limited beam penetration into aggregates, leading to significant errors in predicting supplementary cementitious material content because the device could not adequately detect sand [24].

    Evidence: 1 report

    RCC Chemical Admixture Performance

    Findings from the reports

    • Evaluated ten chemical admixtures in a Roller-Compacted Concrete mixture containing Class F fly ash, finding that they significantly enhanced fresh properties to enable paver compaction and extended the workable time window [19].
    • Demonstrated that polycarboxylate-based water reducers achieved substantial water reduction, offering significant potential for cement savings and improved finishability in Roller-Compacted Concrete [19].
    • Observed that surfactant-based dispersing agents provided notable workability improvements without entraining excessive air, whereas synthetic detergent-type Air-Entraining Admixtures entrained high volumes of air but lacked compactibility retention due to the absence of retarding components [19].
    • Reported that retarders improved later-age strength, although some mixtures exhibited lower early strengths compared to control samples without retarders [19].
    • Showed that the overall behavior of an admixture was critical, requiring a balance between cohesion, segregation resistance, compactibility, finishability, and strength gain rather than focusing on improving a single property [19].

    Evidence: 1 report

    Recycled Concrete Aggregate Mixtures

    Findings from the reports

    • Demonstrated that concrete containing recycled concrete aggregates can meet strength and durability specifications when engineered with optimized water content and supplementary cementitious materials rather than relying on simple replacement of virgin aggregate [4].
    • Revealed that current compliance barriers often stem from prescriptive specifications and testing methods, such as sulfate soundness tests, which do not accurately reflect recycled concrete aggregate performance in freeze-thaw environments or account for its unique porosity and absorption characteristics [4].

    Evidence: 1 report

    Two-Stage Mixing Processes

    Findings from the reports

    • Two-stage mixing processes enhanced concrete performance by improving homogeneity and increasing compressive strength compared to conventional dry-batch methods [5].
    • Laboratory studies demonstrated that two-stage mixed concrete yielded approximately 10 percent higher 28-day compressive strengths than traditional mixes [5].
    • Two-stage mixing produced concrete with greater resistance to chloride penetration and increased tensile strengths relative to conventional ready-mixed methods [5].

    Evidence: 1 report
