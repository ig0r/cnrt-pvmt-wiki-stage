    Key findings

    • The study demonstrated that a 3D structured-light scanning system revealed consistent dimensional deviations in printed concrete parts, including shorter lengths, narrower diameters, and thinner profiles compared to CAD designs [1].
    • The analysis showed that layer widths expanded during the printing process, a pattern attributed primarily to material slump [1].
    • The evaluation found that specimens appearing visually acceptable still exhibited measurable geometric deviations, proving that 3D structured-light scanning provides essential objective insights beyond visual inspection [1].

    Dimensional Deviation Analysis

    Dimensional deviation analysis involves quantifying the geometric differences between a physical object and its intended design specifications. This process relies on high-fidelity three-dimensional surface models generated by structured light scanning, which captures precise measurements of features like height, diameter, and layer thickness [1]. By comparing these empirically captured dimensions against the digital model, analysts can identify specific deviations in form and structure.

    Research problem — There is a lack of reliable, quantitative methods for evaluating the geometric fidelity and quality of 3D printed concrete components [1]. Printed parts frequently exhibit dimensional discrepancies between their measured physical geometry and intended design specifications due to factors such as material slump, which compromises structural performance and fit-up requirements [1].

    State of the art — The current state-of-the-art practice employs structured-light scanning to generate high-resolution point clouds that are sliced into 2-D cross-sections for extracting precise geometric metrics such as height, diameter, and layer thickness [1]. Quality control is achieved by quantitatively comparing these measured values against the original CAD model to identify defects like slump or filament widening with sub-millimeter accuracy [1].

    Findings from the reports

    • The 3D structured-light scanning system revealed consistent dimensional deviations in all printed samples, demonstrating that even visually acceptable parts exhibited measurable geometric errors [1].
    • Quantitative analysis showed that printed concrete parts were systematically shorter and had reduced outer diameters compared to their CAD designs [1].
    • The scanning system observed that layer thickness was reduced while layer widths increased, a pattern attributed primarily to slump during the printing process [1].
    • Evaluation of geometric parameters found that no sample met any of its designed values, indicating imperfect cylindrical shapes and inconsistent layering [1].

    Novelty & rigor — The research introduces a novel workflow that couples structured-light scanning with automated point cloud slicing to extract precise geometric parameters for dimensional deviation analysis [1]. This approach provides a repeatable, quantitative metric for assessing print fidelity by directly comparing extracted features against the original CAD design, moving beyond subjective visual ranking [1]. The methodology is reproducible through standardized specimen production and curing protocols followed by consistent scanning and cross-sectional measurement procedures [1].

    Implications — Objective scanning data is necessary to identify systematic dimensional deviations that visual inspection cannot detect [1].

    Limitations — Dimensional deviations are primarily driven by material behavior, such as insufficient early stiffness leading to slump or distortion during printing, rather than limitations inherent to the scanning system itself [1]. Even when parts appear visually acceptable, they may still contain measurable dimensional errors in height, diameter, layer thickness, and width relative to the intended geometry [1].
