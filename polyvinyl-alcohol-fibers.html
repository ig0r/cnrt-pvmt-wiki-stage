    Polyvinyl Alcohol Fibers

    bridge-decksdurabilityfiber-reinforced-concretefibers
    Also known as: PVA fibers
    Created 2026-07-22 · Updated 2026-08-17T02:09:08Z

    Polyvinyl alcohol fibers are synthetic polymer filaments composed of repeating vinyl alcohol units, originally developed as a substitute for asbestos. These fibers exhibit hydrophilic characteristics and possess noncircular cross-sections that facilitate extensive hydrogen bonding with cementitious matrices [1]. This strong chemical and mechanical adhesion promotes fiber rupture over pull-out failure, resulting in a slip-hardening response within the composite material [1]. The inherent water absorption capacity of polyvinyl alcohol fibers can influence the workability of the surrounding concrete mixture upon addition [1].

    PVA Fiber Mechanical and Fresh Properties

    Polyvinyl alcohol fibers are synthetic, hydrophilic filaments characterized by a noncircular cross-section that promotes extensive hydrogen bonding with the surrounding cementitious matrix [1]. The inherent water absorption capacity of these hydrophilic fibers can significantly influence the fresh state workability of the concrete mixture upon addition [1].

    Research problem — The high water-absorption tendency of PVA macrofibers significantly reduces the workability of fresh concrete mixes, often necessitating additional admixtures or higher water-to-cement ratios to maintain flow [1]. At elevated fiber volumes, the material tends to re-aggregate into clumps, which compromises uniform distribution and jeopardizes proper mixing, placement, and compaction during large-scale construction [1].

    State of the art — PVA fibers are utilized as high-strength synthetic monofilaments that form hydrogenic bonds with cement paste, resulting in adhesion approximately eight times stronger than polypropylene fibers and a slip-hardening response characterized by fiber rupture rather than pull-out [1]. While this mechanism delivers superior post-crack flexural toughness and residual strength at low volume fractions, the fibers’ hydrophilic nature causes significant fresh-state workability reduction due to water absorption [1].

    Findings from the reports

    • PVA macrofibers markedly reduced workability due to high water absorption, increasing the demand for water-reducing admixtures [1].
    • PVA fibers demonstrated superior chemical adhesion to cement through hydrogen bonding, resulting in bond strength approximately eight times that of polypropylene fibers and causing fiber rupture rather than pull-out [1].
    • The study showed that PVA provided tensile performance comparable to alkali-resistant glass fibers and superior post-peak flexural behavior relative to polypropylene fibers [1].
    • At dosages of 0.1875%–0.25%, PVA macrofibers produced noticeable post-peak flexural strength and toughness, delivering up to a 27% increase in flexural strength relative to the control mix [1].
    • Hybrid mixes containing PVA at a dosage of 0.25% achieved higher flexural strength than those with polypropylene or alkali-resistant glass fibers [1].

    Novelty & rigor — The research introduces a hybrid fiber-reinforced concrete system that strategically combines polyvinyl alcohol macrofibers with polypropylene microfibers to exploit the chemical affinity between PVA and the cement matrix [1]. This approach is distinct because it leverages strong hydrogenic bonding to shift failure modes from fiber pull-out to rupture, thereby achieving superior post-peak toughness at low dosages that are atypical for conventional synthetic-fiber mixes [1]. The methodology is reproducible through a standardized three-stage mixing sequence and specific admixture adjustments to counteract the water-absorbing nature of PVA fibers, ensuring consistent workability and mechanical performance benchmarks for bridge-deck applications [1].

    Implications — Designers should restrict PVA macrofibers in bridge-deck concrete where balanced workability and compressive strength are critical, favoring alkali-resistant glass fibers instead [1]. PVA fibers are best reserved for applications prioritizing durability and shrinkage-crack control, provided the mix is engineered with appropriate superplasticizers to offset reduced workability [1].

    Limitations — The high water absorption capacity of polyvinyl alcohol fibers significantly reduces fresh-state workability and increases the demand for chemical admixtures, complicating mix handling [1]. Incorporation of these macrofibers tends to decrease compressive strength compared to control mixes or those reinforced with alkali-resistant glass fibers [1]. Practical application is hindered by higher costs, limited availability, and mixing difficulties such as fiber clumping when volumes exceed specific thresholds [1].
