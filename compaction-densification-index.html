    Compaction Densification Index

    compactibilityconceptindexrcc
    Also known as: CDI
    Created 2026-05-20 · Updated 2026-08-16T09:02:38Z

    The Compaction Densification Index is a metric that quantifies the additional compaction effort required to transition a pervious concrete mix from its initial workable state to a target design void content. By expressing this extra energy requirement, the index helps engineers assess how much further compaction is needed beyond the initial placement to meet performance specifications [2].

    Compaction Densification Index Definition and Trends

    The Compaction Densification Index serves as a quantitative metric that evaluates the additional compactive effort necessary to transition a pervious concrete mixture from its initial workable state toward a target design void content [2]. This index is derived by calculating the area under the gyratory compaction curve within the post-workability region, effectively measuring the energy required to achieve specific density specifications beyond initial placement [2].

    Research problem — Conventional compactibility tests lack the discrimination required to detect subtle density variations in roller-compacted concrete, leading to non-uniform densification and compromised pavement performance [1]. Traditional slump testing fails to provide a reliable metric for quantifying the workability and compaction density of pervious concrete, leaving engineers without a practical tool to predict field performance or prevent over- and under-compaction [2].

    State of the art — This index quantifies the additional energy required after initial workability to reach target density and is used alongside the Workability Energy Index to form a placeability assessment for pervious concrete mixtures [2].

    Findings from the reports

    • Defined the Compaction Densification Index (CDI) as a measure of extra compaction energy required after initial workability, calculated either from five to twenty gyrations or from eight gyrations to design void content [1] [2].
    • Demonstrated that CDI declines sharply as the binder-to-aggregate ratio increases, reaching zero at high ratios where no additional compaction energy beyond initial gyrations is needed [2].
    • Observed that increasing the water-to-cement ratio significantly reduces CDI, indicating lower resistance to further compaction as fluidity increases [2].
    • Found that incorporating fibers increased resistance to compaction, resulting in a higher CDI compared to non-fiber mixes due to reduced workability [2].
    • Reported that longer mixing times reduced both workability and CDI, causing the mix to stiffen and require additional compaction effort [2].
    • Showed that high-dosage water reducers at upper levels could prevent obtaining compactibility indices at early measurement times, though most admixtures retained good compactibility at later intervals [1].

    Novelty & rigor — The Compaction Densification Index is introduced as a novel quantitative metric that isolates the additional densification effort required beyond initial compaction stages by calculating the area under or above relative density curves within specific gyration ranges [1] [2]. This approach provides a distinct and reproducible framework for evaluating mixture compactibility by converting gyratory compactor data into objective indices that are more discriminating than traditional slump or Vebe tests [1] [2].

    Across the reviewed reports, the Compaction Densification Index is defined as a measure of compaction energy or area under the density curve within specific gyration ranges, though the exact boundaries vary between studies. While one report notes that high-dosage water reducers can impair compactibility at early stages but show good retention later, another establishes that CDI declines sharply as binder-to-aggregate ratios increase and remains low for higher water content. Both reports indicate that factors such as fiber incorporation or extended mixing times can stiffen the mix and increase resistance to compaction, thereby raising the CDI value compared to baseline conditions. [1] [2]

    Implications — Designers should calibrate mixture proportions to specific compaction methods by targeting binder-to-aggregate ratios above 19% and limiting water-cement ratios near 0.27, as these parameters significantly reduce the energy required for densification [2]. Construction practices must minimize mixing delays to prevent increased stiffness and compaction effort, ensuring that workability is maintained within defined indices to meet void-content specifications without risking rejection [2].

    Limitations — The available literature does not explicitly acknowledge specific limitations or caveats regarding the definition and trends of the Compaction Densification Index [2].
