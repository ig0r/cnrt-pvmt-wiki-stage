    Infrared Thermography

    ndtnondestructive-testingpavement-evaluationthermal
    Also known as: thermal imaging
    Created 2026-05-20 · Updated 2026-08-17T17:32:10Z

    Infrared thermography is a non-destructive testing technique that visualizes surface temperature distributions to identify subsurface anomalies. The method operates on the principle that internal defects, such as delaminations or voids, disrupt normal heat flow patterns within a material [1]. These disruptions create detectable thermal contrasts on the exterior surface that correspond to the horizontal extent of the underlying flaws [1]. While effective for locating lateral defect boundaries, the technique does not directly quantify the depth or vertical dimensions of the anomalies [1].

    Infrared Thermography Applications and Constraints

    Infrared thermography is a non-destructive evaluation technique that maps surface temperature distributions to identify subsurface anomalies by detecting disruptions in heat flow [1]. This method is widely applied to detect defects such as delaminations within concrete infrastructure, including bridge decks [1]. While the technique effectively reveals the horizontal extent of a flaw, it does not directly quantify the depth or vertical dimensions of the underlying anomaly [1].

    Research problem — Infrared thermography faces significant constraints in detecting subsurface defects located near the base of concrete pavement slabs, as its effectiveness is heavily influenced by ambient conditions and it lacks the ability to quantify anomaly depth or thickness [1]. There is a critical need for reliable non-destructive methods to identify hidden delamination and assess bond quality in Portland-cement-concrete overlays, since undetected defects can lead to premature pavement failure and costly repairs [2].

    State of the art — Infrared thermography is an established nondestructive evaluation method for concrete structures, standardized by ASTM D 4788 and routinely applied to infrastructure such as bridge decks, highways, dams, and airport taxiways [1]. Recent advances in computer hardware and software have significantly simplified the acquisition, processing, and visualization of thermographic data [1]. Despite its utility as a rapid global inspection tool, the technique is constrained by sensitivity to ambient conditions and lacks the ability to directly quantify the depth or thickness of subsurface anomalies [1].

    Limitations — The high cost of equipment and the method’s sensitivity to ambient conditions restrict its operational feasibility [1]. Inability to quantify subsurface flaw depth or thickness prevents accurate defect localization near the slab base [1]. Reliable results depend heavily on operator experience, making the method less suitable than ground-penetrating radar for detecting materials-related distress [1].
