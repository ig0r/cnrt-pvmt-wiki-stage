    Sorptivity

    concretecuringdurabilitytesting
    Also known as: sorptivity coefficient, water absorption
    Created 2026-05-20 · Updated 2026-08-17T14:10:03Z

    Sorptivity is a material property that quantifies the rate at which water is drawn into a porous medium by capillary action. It is typically expressed as a coefficient derived from the slope of cumulative water uptake versus the square root of time, reflecting the combined influence of pore size distribution and connectivity on moisture transport [2] [3] [6]. The magnitude of sorptivity depends on the material’s existing moisture condition and its internal microstructure, such as pore shape and connectivity [1]. In cementitious systems, ongoing hydration progressively fills pores with products that reduce water absorption, making sorptivity a useful indicator of curing quality and durability against moisture-induced deterioration [1] [2].

    Key findings

    • Sorptivity proved to be the most responsive indicator of how curing compounds modify the near-surface microstructure, with high-efficiency resin-based formulations producing the lowest values [1].
    • Double-layer applications of curing compounds consistently produced lower sorptivity values than single layers, indicating a finer pore structure despite unchanged moisture readings [1].
    • Sorptivity correlated linearly with moisture content and the degree of cement hydration, confirming that curing compounds raise moisture and hydration levels while reducing sorptivity [1].
    • Concrete specimens immersed in de-icing liquid showed reduced initial and secondary water absorption rates compared with plain-water cured samples, indicating a less permeable surface [5].
    • De-icing salts slowed concrete’s sorption behavior by raising surface tension, viscosity, and density while lowering water activity, resulting in reduced absorption rates and total amounts absorbed [7].
    • Penetrating sealers markedly lowered concrete sorptivity, with the silane formulation producing the greatest reduction in both initial and secondary water absorption and delaying saturation for well over three months [4].
    • Higher water contact angles produced by penetrating sealers were directly linked to reduced capillary suction, confirming that increased hydrophosity translates into lower sorptivity [4].
    • Surface densifiers showed little to no effect on sorptivity, performing similarly to untreated concrete [4].

    Surface Treatments, Curing, and Overlay Effects on Sorptivity

    Sorptivity quantifies the rate at which water is drawn into a porous material by capillary action, serving as a key indicator of moisture transport and durability [1] [3] [6]. This property depends on pore structure characteristics such as size, shape, and connectivity, which are progressively modified by ongoing hydration during curing [1] [2]. Surface treatments and overlays alter the near-surface microstructure and pore connectivity, thereby influencing the material’s ability to absorb water and its resistance to moisture-induced deterioration [1] [2].

    Research problem — There is a lack of reliable standard testing methods to evaluate the effectiveness of field concrete curing, creating a need for sensitive indicators that can verify whether curing compounds are performing as intended [1]. Conventional acceptance criteria fail to reliably predict long-term pavement performance, necessitating transport property tests like sorptivity to provide direct indicators of fluid ingress and durability risks such as chloride penetration and freeze-thaw damage [3]. Low-modulus concrete overlays exhibit significantly higher secondary sorptivity than the high-performance substrates they replace, leading to increased moisture ingress and vulnerability to deterioration processes that compromise service life [2].

    State of the art — Sorptivity is widely recognized as a highly sensitive laboratory indicator for assessing near-surface concrete quality, capturing subtle variations in pore structure and moisture conditions that conventional strength tests often miss [1]. The standard evaluation method relies on ASTM C1585 to measure capillary water uptake over time, deriving distinct initial and secondary sorptivity slopes that reflect early-age moisture ingress and long-term durability trends [2] [3].

    Findings from the reports

    • Penetrating sealers markedly lowered concrete sorptivity by increasing surface hydrophobicity, with silane and pore-blocking formulations producing the largest reductions in capillary suction [4].
    • Surface densifiers showed little to no effect on sorptivity, performing similarly to untreated concrete [4].
    • Adding superabsorbent polymers increased both initial and secondary water absorption rates, indicating higher capillary suction due to polymer-induced porosity [6].
    • Very-early-strength low-modulus concrete overlays exhibited lower initial sorptivity than high-performance concrete substrates but showed elevated secondary sorptivity during early service that converged over time as pore networks refined [2].
    • Curing compounds significantly reduced near-surface sorptivity, with double-layer applications and high-efficiency resin-based products yielding the lowest values [1].
    • Sorptivity correlated linearly with moisture content and degree of cement hydration, confirming that curing practices alter microstructure to reduce water absorption potential [1].

    Novelty & rigor — Research has shifted from treating sorptivity as a peripheral metric to using it as the primary quantitative gauge for evaluating curing effectiveness and sealer performance, revealing subtle microstructural refinements that conventional moisture or strength tests miss [1]. Novel methodological approaches integrate wettability measurements with detailed sorptivity assessments to predict durability indicators like time to critical saturation, and link internal-curing polymer kinetics directly to capillary suction changes in modified concrete mixes [4] [6]. Distinctive testing protocols now treat overlays as living materials by tracking longitudinal sorptivity evolution through field-core extraction and data-driven curve fitting, while standardized reproduction requires strict adherence to specific mix proportions, curing environments, and specimen preparation details [1] [2].

Specific formulations demonstrate distinct efficacy, with high-efficiency resin-based curing compounds and silane sealers yielding the lowest sorptivity values compared to water-based or less effective alternatives. Overlay applications also modify sorptivity profiles, exhibiting lower initial absorption rates than substrates but higher secondary sorptivity that converges over time as pore networks refine. [1] [2] [4] [6]

    Implications — Specification agencies should prioritize penetrating sealers that suppress sorptivity while preserving pore connectivity, as durability assessments must rely on desaturation or saturation-delay tests rather than simple air-permeability measurements [4]. The use of superabsorbent polymers for internal curing requires careful matching of polymer type and dosage to the mix design to mitigate the resulting increase in sorptivity and avoid compromising long-term durability [6]. Designers must implement measures such as supplementary cementitious materials and rigorous early-age curing to lower secondary sorptivity in lime-modified concrete overlays, thereby mitigating vulnerability to shrinkage cracking during the first months of service [2]. Practitioners should adopt sorptivity as the primary field metric for evaluating curing effectiveness and select high-efficiency compounds applied in double layers under hot weather conditions to ensure superior microstructural quality [1].

    Limitations — The applicability of the findings is restricted to the specific mix designs, material types, and dosages evaluated, preventing generalization to other concrete formulations [6]. Methodological constraints include subjective selection of linear-fit regions for slope calculation and standardized measurement windows that may fail to capture long-term moisture transport behavior [1] [2]. Laboratory protocols utilizing accelerated conditions and single exposure scenarios do not fully replicate complex field environments or account for the inconsistent correlation between surface treatments and secondary absorption rates [4].

    Environmental Exposure: Deicing Salt Effects

    Research problem — High sorptivity accelerates moisture penetration into concrete, which exacerbates freeze-thaw damage and surface scaling in cold climates [5]. Deicing salts alter pore solution properties to reduce sorptivity, a change that paradoxically increases saturation at joints and promotes microcracking and premature deterioration [7].

    State of the art — Current practice evaluates the sorptivity of concrete using standardized testing methods like ASTM C1585 on various surface types, supplemented by thermodynamic modeling to link equilibrium relative humidity with solution properties and pore structure [7]. Recent advancements recognize that deicing salts significantly alter liquid viscosity, surface tension, water activity, and density, which collectively reduce absorption rates and increase the material’s degree of saturation in salt-exposed concrete pavements [7].

    Findings from the reports

    • Concrete immersed in Ossian Season One deicing liquid exhibited reduced initial and secondary water absorption rates compared to plain-water cured controls, indicating a denser surface layer and improved impermeability [5].
    • The addition of deicing salts lowered both the rate and total amount of water absorbed by concrete, with absorption declining in proportion to the square-root of the surface-tension-to-viscosity ratio as salt concentration increased [7].
    • Deicing salts fundamentally altered fluid properties by raising surface tension, viscosity, and density while lowering water activity, which collectively slowed concrete’s sorption behavior [7].

    Novelty & rigor — The research introduces a novel application of sorptivity testing to evaluate the sealing effects of liquid de-icing products, specifically by measuring water absorption rates in specimens immersed directly in the treatment solution rather than plain water [5]. An innovative methodological approach couples standard sorption measurements with a thermodynamic model that explicitly accounts for how de-icing salts alter fluid properties such as viscosity and surface tension, linking these changes to the concrete’s pore structure and equilibrium relative humidity [7]. The experimental protocols are reproducible through detailed specifications for concrete mix designs, curing regimes, and conditioning procedures that allow for consistent comparison of initial and secondary absorption rates across different de-icing agents [5] [7].

    Across the reviewed reports, deicing salts consistently reduce concrete sorptivity by altering fluid properties such as surface tension and viscosity, which slows both initial absorption rates and total water uptake compared to plain water controls. While one study attributes the reduced sorption in specific deicing liquids to the formation of a denser, less permeable surface layer, another establishes that this effect scales with salt concentration and is governed by the square-root relationship between surface tension and viscosity. Both reports converge on the finding that deicing salts lower sorptivity, though they differ in describing the resulting state: one emphasizes an improved impermeable barrier structure, while the other highlights increased saturation retention during desorption due to the hygroscopic nature of the salts. [5] [7]

    Implications — Designers must account for the fact that de-icing salts alter concrete sorptivity by reducing water uptake rates while simultaneously increasing equilibrium moisture content due to hygroscopic effects [7]. Specifying sealants is particularly valuable in these environments because they can block salt ingress and help maintain lower joint saturation levels despite the modified sorption characteristics [7].
