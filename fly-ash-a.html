    Fly Ash

    fly-ashmaterialspozzolanscm
    Also known as: FA, pulverized fuel ash
    Created 2026-05-20 · Updated 2026-08-14T19:37:58Z

    Fly ash is a fine, spherical by-product of coal combustion captured from flue gases that serves as a supplementary cementitious material in concrete. It is classified into Class F and Class C based on its chemical composition, with the former being low-calcium and primarily pozzolanic, while the latter contains higher calcium levels that impart hydraulic or self-cementing properties [1] [4] [18] [23]. When incorporated into concrete, fly ash partially replaces Portland cement and reacts with calcium hydroxide released during hydration to form additional calcium silicate hydrate gel [4] [11] [29]. This pozzolanic reaction refines the pore structure, reduces permeability, and enhances long-term strength and durability while mitigating deleterious reactions such as alkali-silica expansion [1] [19] [23]. The spherical morphology of the particles acts to reduce internal friction and water demand, thereby improving workability and reducing heat evolution during the early stages of cement hydration [2] [11] [36].

    Key findings

    • Low-calcium Class F fly ash significantly lowers the heat of hydration and delays setting times, whereas high-calcium Class C fly ash can generate excess heat and behave more like ordinary Portland cement [4] [17] [18].
    • Low-calcium Class F fly ash effectively mitigates alkali-silica reaction (ASR) and improves sulfate resistance, while high-calcium Class C fly ash often exhibits poor sulfate performance and may exacerbate ASR unless used at high replacement levels with other pozzolans [5] [19] [23].
    • Incorporating fly ash reduces the peak temperature of hydration, delays its occurrence, and introduces a distinct secondary heat-evolution event observable in isothermal calorimetry [4] [16] [18].
    • Fly ash enhances fresh-state workability by reducing viscosity and yield stress, which lowers water demand due to the spherical shape of its particles [2] [14] [35].
    • Early-age strength development is markedly delayed for both Class C and Class F fly ash mixes, although strengths typically converge or exceed control values at later ages (e.g., 28 days) [4] [18] [19].
    • Higher proportions of fly ash generally decrease warping and curling in concrete pavements, although excessive amounts may increase water demand if slump is maintained [12] [22].
    • Fly ash refines the pore structure of concrete, resulting in more hydrophobic surfaces and improved resistance to deicing salts by reducing calcium oxychloride formation [15] [25].

    Fly Ash As Supplementary Cementitious Material

    Fly ash is a fine, spherical by-product of coal combustion that serves as a supplementary cementitious material to partially replace Portland cement in concrete mixtures [1] [23]. It is classified into Class F and Class C based on its calcium oxide content, with the former acting primarily as a pozzolan and the latter exhibiting both pozzolanic and hydraulic properties [1] [23]. The material refines the pore structure by reacting with calcium hydroxide to form additional cementitious gel, which enhances long-term strength and durability while reducing permeability [11] [24]. Its spherical morphology improves fresh mix workability and reduces water demand, while its slower reaction kinetics lower the heat of hydration and mitigate early-age thermal cracking [17] [36].

    Research problem — The inherent chemical variability of fly ash, particularly the distinct behaviors of Class C versus Class F materials, creates classification and sourcing challenges that can lead to unpredictable concrete performance and durability failures [1] [5] [19]. Incorporating fly ash introduces complex fresh-state and early-age hydration issues, including workability instability, set-time retardation or acceleration, and reduced early strength, which complicate mix design reliability and construction scheduling [14] [17] [18] [23]. There is a critical need to determine optimal replacement dosages that balance environmental benefits with performance requirements, as excessive or insufficient fly ash can negatively impact shrinkage, thermal cracking risk, and long-term durability mechanisms such as freeze-thaw resistance [12] [24] [29] [34].

    State of the art — Fly ash is a standard supplementary cementitious material in concrete pavements, typically replacing 15–30% of Portland cement to enhance durability and reduce carbon emissions [1] [4]. Modern mix designs increasingly rely on performance-based specifications and ternary blends with slag or silica fume rather than prescriptive limits, as the varying chemistry of Class F and Class C ashes requires careful selection to balance workability, heat evolution, and long-term strength [1] [4] [6]. Current quality control practices utilize calorimetry and chemical characterization to monitor hydration rates and set times, ensuring that the delayed strength gain associated with fly ash is managed through optimized admixture dosages and mixing protocols [16] [18].

    Findings from the reports

    • Fly ash consistently improved fresh-state workability by lowering viscosity and yield stress, acting as a water reducer that allowed for higher slump or reduced water demand [2] [4] [11] [35].
    • Fly ash significantly altered the hydration kinetics by lowering and delaying the peak heat evolution, extending both initial and final set times relative to plain Portland cement [4] [16] [17] [18].
    • Fly ash reduced early-age compressive strength but enhanced long-term strength, with many mixes achieving equal or superior performance at 28 days and beyond compared to control mixes [4] [14] [18].
    • Fly ash reduced the heat of hydration, which helped mitigate thermal cracking risks and lowered peak temperatures in mass concrete applications [4] [17] [35].
    • The addition of fly ash generally reduced drying shrinkage and plastic shrinkage cracking, particularly when combined with specific admixtures or expansive cements [2] [4] [11].
    • Incorporating fly ash lowered the permeability of concrete and improved resistance to chloride ion penetration over time, enhancing long-term durability [13] [14] [15].
    • Low-calcium Class F fly ash effectively mitigated alkali-silica reaction (ASR) and sulfate expansion, whereas high-calcium Class C fly ash often performed poorly in these durability tests [19] [23].
    • Fly ash exhibited broader chemical variability than Portland cement due to its nature as a by-product, though it consistently met standard specification limits for major oxides [4] [5].
    • Higher replacement levels of fly ash (up to 35%) were associated with increased curling and warping in concrete slabs, although moderate dosages often reduced these deformations [12] [22].
    • Fly ash provided a low embodied carbon alternative to Portland cement, significantly reducing the overall carbon intensity of concrete mixtures [19] [35].

    Novelty & rigor — The research introduces a systematic optimization framework that utilizes rapid, low-material paste and mortar screening to pinpoint optimal fly ash dosages before full-scale concrete casting, effectively linking detailed material assay with mechanistic insights into false-set mitigation [11]. Distinctive methodologies include the development of a weighted average calcium-oxide content variable to predict heat-signature parameters across ternary systems and the application of high-shear two-stage mixing regimes that reduce paste viscosity and energy requirements [4] [14]. Reproducibility is supported by rigorous protocols involving specific chemical characterization via X-ray fluorescence, standardized mixing procedures, and the use of semi-adiabatic or isothermal calorimetry to capture distinct hydration signatures for field monitoring [5] [16] [18].

    Across the reviewed reports, incorporating fly ash as a supplementary cementitious material consistently enhances fresh-state workability by reducing viscosity and water demand, while simultaneously delaying early-age hydration through extended setting times and reduced heat evolution. While Class F fly ash reliably mitigates alkali-silica reaction and sulfate expansion, high-calcium Class C fly ash exhibits variable durability performance that can be inferior to Class F in these specific chemical resistance tests. Long-term mechanical properties generally show modest early-age strength reductions followed by significant gains at later ages, with some studies indicating that higher replacement levels can increase slab curling and warping tendencies. [4] [17] [18] [19] [22] [23]

    Implications — Specifications should mandate the use of Class F fly ash to mitigate alkali-silica reactivity and sulfate attack, while restricting or carefully controlling Class C ash in aggressive environments due to its potential to weaken sulfate resistance [19] [23]. Designers must account for the thermal inertia of fly ash by adjusting construction schedules and curing protocols to accommodate delayed setting times and slower early-age strength development, particularly in low ambient temperatures [16] [18]. Quality control programs should implement batch-by-batch screening using isothermal calorimetry to verify source consistency and predict heat evolution profiles, ensuring that variability in ash composition does not compromise dimensional stability or durability targets [5] [16] [18].

    Limitations — The chemical and physical variability of fly ash necessitates project-specific screening for compatibility, as certain classes have demonstrated poor sulfate resistance or incompatibility with specific admixtures [19]. Laboratory-derived durability thresholds and strength models may not directly translate to field conditions due to the influence of climatic variations, construction inconsistencies, and limited experimental matrices [4] [6] [9] [13]. Specific performance trade-offs include reduced early-age green strength in self-consolidating concrete and potential retardation of early strengths, while calorimetric testing methods may fail to accurately capture fly ash hydration effects [16] [35].
