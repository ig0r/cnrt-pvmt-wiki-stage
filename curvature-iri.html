    Curvature IRI

    curling-indicatoriripavement-measurementroughness
    Also known as: curvature-related IRI
    Created 2026-07-22 · Updated 2026-08-16T12:25:16Z

    Curvature IRI is a specialized metric derived from the International Roughness Index that isolates and quantifies pavement roughness specifically caused by slab curling or warping. The index is calculated by processing longitudinal or transverse elevation data to fit each concrete slab to a quadratic curve, thereby extracting the deformation-induced component of surface irregularity [1] [2]. A mathematical vehicle model simulates tire response to these undulations at standard travel speeds to convert the curvature profile into a single roughness value [1] [2]. Higher values indicate greater deviation from flatness due to slab bending, while lower values correspond to smoother pavements with minimal warping effects [1] [2].

    Key findings

    • Curvature IRI consistently declines as the total equivalent temperature difference approaches zero, reaching near-zero values when that difference is close to 0 °F [1].
    • The shrinkage-related equivalent temperature difference exerts the strongest influence on curvature IRI, outweighing both hourly and overall temperature effects [1].
    • Curvature IRI exhibits seasonal trends with the greatest values occurring in fall and the smallest in spring [1].
    • Narrower slabs, thinner sections, skewed joints, larger joint spacing, untied PCC shoulders, and high-strength mixes increase curvature IRI [1].
    • Wider slabs, greater thickness, rectangular joints, tied shoulders, larger dowel bar diameters, and low-strength QMC mixes reduce curvature IRI [1].
    • Curvature IRI behaves similarly to traditional IRI but is strongly influenced by joint spacing rather than overlay thickness or fiber reinforcement [2].
    • Sections with short transverse joints (5.5–6 ft) consistently produce significantly higher curvature IRI values compared to sections with longer joint spacings [2].
    • Seasonal or time-of-day testing conditions have minimal impact on Curvature IRI, indicating that built-in curling and warping drive the observed roughness rather than external factors [2].

    Curvature IRI Characteristics

    Curvature IRI is a specialized metric derived from pavement profile measurements that isolates the ride-quality degradation caused specifically by slab curling and warping [1] [2]. The index quantifies this deformation-induced roughness by fitting elevation data to a quadratic curve and simulating vehicle response using a quarter-car model [1] [2]. Higher values indicate greater deviation from a flat surface due to curvature effects, while lower values correspond to smoother pavements with less warping [1] [2].

    Research problem — There is currently no standardized quantitative method for isolating the roughness attributable to slab curling and warping from other distress sources using curvature-based International Roughness Index [1]. The lack of clear, repeatable patterns in curvature IRI with respect to key design parameters such as joint spacing and overlay thickness hinders reliable interpretation and limits its utility for guiding pavement design decisions [2].

    State of the art — Current practice identifies Curvature IRI as the primary quantitative indicator for concrete-slab curling and warping, derived from high-resolution surface measurements processed through sophisticated algorithms to isolate temperature- or moisture-induced deformation [1] [2].

    Findings from the reports

    • Curvature IRI declined as the total equivalent temperature difference approached zero, with shrinkage-related temperature differences exerting a stronger influence than hourly or overall temperature effects [1].
    • Narrower and thinner slabs, untied shoulders, high-strength mixes, and skewed joints increased curvature IRI, whereas wider/thicker slabs, tied shoulders, larger dowel bars, and low-strength mixes reduced it [1].
    • Curvature IRI exhibited seasonal variation with higher values in fall and lower values in spring, while diurnal peaks occurred in the early morning compared to lower afternoon values [1].
    • Transverse joint spacing emerged as the primary driver of curvature IRI variability across test sites, with significantly higher roughness observed at shorter spacings (e.g., 6 ft) compared to longer intervals [2].
    • Overlay thickness and fiber reinforcement exerted secondary or negligible influence on curvature IRI compared to the dominant effect of joint spacing [2].
    • Seasonal and time-of-day testing conditions had minimal impact on curvature IRI, indicating that built-in curling and warping rather than external environmental factors drove the observed roughness [2].

    Novelty & rigor — The research introduces a novel, data-driven workflow that isolates the roughness component attributable specifically to concrete slab curling and warping by coupling Second-Generation Curvature Index modeling with second-order polynomial fitting [1]. This approach uniquely decouples curvature-related deformation from general ride quality metrics, enabling objective, automated assessment of pavement distress independent of traditional International Roughness Index values [2]. The methodology is highly reproducible through a standardized protocol involving calibrated high-speed inertial profiling and specific MATLAB-based post-processing algorithms that consistently yield curvature metrics across varying seasonal and geometric conditions [1] [2].

    Across the reviewed reports, curvature IRI is primarily driven by geometric and design characteristics rather than external environmental fluctuations, with transverse joint spacing identified as a critical determinant of roughness magnitude. While shorter joint spacings significantly increase curvature IRI values compared to longer intervals, other construction variables such as slab thickness and dowel bar diameter also exert measurable influence on the index’s baseline levels. The indicator demonstrates a strong correlation with temperature differentials, declining toward zero as equivalent temperature differences approach zero, yet it remains largely insensitive to diurnal or seasonal time-of-day variations once built-in curling is established. [1] [2]

    Implications — Designers should prioritize minimizing temperature differentials through quality concrete mixes, rectangular slab geometries with tied shoulders, adequate joint spacing, and sufficient thickness to reduce curvature IRI [1]. Post-construction diamond grinding is essential to achieve acceptable ride quality, as its absence significantly increases curvature IRI regardless of overlay thickness or fiber reinforcement [2]. While shorter transverse joint spacing can modestly increase curvature IRI, design emphasis should remain on appropriate spacing and post-construction smoothing rather than restricting spacing solely for smoothness concerns [2].

    Limitations — The provisional nature of the conclusions is driven by a lack of standardized techniques for describing environmental effects and insufficient Phase I data to fully validate recommendations [1]. Predictive accuracy is constrained by omitted variables, unmodeled factors like temperature-moisture gradients, and measurement errors in joint detection and manual visual checks [1]. Reliability is further limited by confounding effects from maintenance activities, low sensitivity to temporal variations, and inconsistent patterns across different design parameters [2].
