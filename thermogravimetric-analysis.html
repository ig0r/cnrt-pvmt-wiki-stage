    Thermogravimetric Analysis

    chemistryconcretematerialstesting
    Also known as: TGA
    Created 2026-05-20 · Updated 2026-08-16T05:24:03Z

    Thermogravimetric analysis is an analytical technique that measures the change in mass of a sample as it is heated at a controlled rate within a defined atmosphere. The method continuously records weight loss versus temperature to identify specific thermal events such as decomposition, oxidation, or the release of volatile components [1] [3]. By analyzing distinct mass-loss regions corresponding to characteristic decomposition temperatures, the technique allows for the identification and quantification of different material phases and their thermal stability [2] [3].

    Key findings

    • Thermogravimetric analysis distinguished specific hydration phases by linking distinct weight-loss regions to free water evaporation, calcium hydroxide decomposition, and C-S-H gel breakdown [1].
    • The degree of hydration derived from thermogravimetric analysis showed a strong linear correlation with conventional loss-on-ignition methods, establishing the technique as a reliable alternative for assessing cement hydration [1].
    • Thermogravimetric analysis demonstrated that incorporating superabsorbent polymers increased the total bound water content in cement paste compared to control mixes [2].
    • The increase in bound water observed with superabsorbent polymer addition corresponded to a modest rise in calcium hydroxide content, confirming that internal curing promoted additional hydrate formation [2].

    Cement Hydration Kinetics and Product Evolution

    Cement hydration involves the reaction of clinker phases with water to form distinct crystalline and amorphous hydrates, such as calcium silicate hydrate gel, ettringite, AFm phases, and portlandite [2]. These hydration products possess characteristic thermal stabilities that result in specific mass loss events when the material is heated through defined temperature intervals [1] [3]. The evolution of these phases over time dictates the overall degree of reaction and the resulting chemical composition of the hardened cement paste [2].

    Research problem — The high cost and specialized training required for Thermogravimetric Analysis create accessibility barriers in concrete laboratories, while the technique’s systematic underestimation of hydration levels risks leading to incorrect conclusions about curing effectiveness [1]. It remains unclear whether TGA can reliably predict field performance metrics such as freeze-thaw durability or accurately quantify the influence of admixtures on bound water and hydrate formation without accounting for critical factors like particle strength and matrix interactions [2] [3].

    State of the art — In contemporary research, the technique is often paired with calorimetry to evaluate admixture effects on hydration kinetics [2].

    Findings from the reports

    • The degree of cement hydration derived from TGA showed a strong linear correlation with results from the conventional furnace loss-on-ignition method, demonstrating that both techniques provide consistent assessments despite minor differences in absolute values [1].
    • Thermogravimetric analysis confirmed that TGA serves as a reliable technique for quantifying evaporable water and hydration products, offering a detailed phase-specific alternative to simpler but less informative methods [1].
    • The addition of superabsorbent polymers increased the total amount of chemically bound water in cement paste compared with plain controls, indicating enhanced overall hydration and more uniform moisture distribution [2].
    • Superabsorbent polymer-modified mixes exhibited higher portlandite contents than the control mix, confirming that internal curing promoted additional hydrate formation without altering the fundamental temperature intervals of mass loss [2].

    Novelty & rigor — The research introduces thermogravimetric analysis as a rapid, controlled method for quantifying non-evaporable water to assess the degree of cement hydration influenced by various curing compounds [1]. This approach is validated against traditional furnace techniques through strong linear correlation, establishing it as a faster alternative with comparable accuracy for evaluating curing effectiveness [1]. The study uniquely applies this technique to quantify how super-absorbent polymers affect hydration products by linking mass loss in specific temperature intervals to improved moisture distribution and durability [2].

    Implications — Practitioners can select low-cost furnace testing for routine monitoring of curing effectiveness when resources are limited, while reserving high-precision TGA for detailed analysis [1]. The retention of additional chemically bound water by superabsorbent polymers implies a denser hydrate matrix that enhances durability, despite potential trade-offs in strength and permeability from residual pores [2].

    Limitations — The high cost of equipment and requirement for specialized training limit the accessibility of this technique compared to simpler alternatives [1]. Systematic differences in curve point selection can cause results to diverge slightly from furnace tests, preventing direct interchangeability between the methods [1].
