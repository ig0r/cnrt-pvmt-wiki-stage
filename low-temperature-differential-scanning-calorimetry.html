    Low-Temperature Differential Scanning Calorimetry (LT-DSC)

    analytical-techniquecalcium-oxychlorideconcrete-durabilitytest-method
    Also known as: LT-DSC, differential scanning calorimetry, low-temperature DSC
    Created 2026-07-22 · Updated 2026-08-16T05:28:17Z

    Low-temperature differential scanning calorimetry is a thermal analysis technique designed to monitor heat flow differences between a sample and an inert reference as the system undergoes controlled cooling and heating cycles through sub-zero temperature ranges. The method operates by detecting endothermic or exothermic events that occur during phase transitions, such as freezing or melting, which are recorded as peaks in the heat flow data [2]. By integrating these thermal signals, the technique quantifies the enthalpy associated with specific chemical reactions or phase changes, enabling the identification and measurement of reaction products within complex material systems [2] [3].

    Calcium Oxychloride Quantification

    Low-temperature differential scanning calorimetry is a thermal analysis technique that monitors heat flow differences between a sample and an inert reference as the temperature cycles through sub-zero ranges [2]. The method identifies specific chemical transitions by detecting endothermic or exothermic peaks associated with phase changes, such as freezing or melting events within cementitious materials [2]. Quantification of reaction products like calcium oxychloride is achieved by integrating the enthalpy values derived from these thermal transitions [2] [3].

    Research problem — The formation of expansive calcium oxychloride in concrete exposed to chloride-bearing, low-temperature environments drives premature joint cracking and pavement deterioration [1] [3]. There is a need for rapid, quantitative screening methods to measure calcium oxychloride formation so that engineers can evaluate chemical durability and optimize mixture designs or sealers to suppress this expansive phase [1] [2] [3].

    State of the art — Low-temperature differential scanning calorimetry has emerged as a standard laboratory technique for quantifying calcium oxychloride formation in cementitious materials exposed to deicing salts [2]. The method involves preparing a slurry of ground concrete powder and a calcium chloride solution, then analyzing the thermal cycle to integrate heat-flow data from the oxychloride melting peak for mass fraction calculation [2].

    Findings from the reports

    • LT-DSC demonstrated that a modified M-QMC mix generated significantly less heat during the calcium oxychloride melting phase than a conventional QMC mix, indicating a dramatic reduction in oxychloride formation [2].
    • LT-DSC measurements revealed that every penetrating sealer evaluated lowered the potential for calcium oxychloride formation relative to an untreated control after immersion in a calcium chloride solution [1].
    • The study found that surface-densifier sealers achieved the greatest reduction in calcium oxychloride amounts, while acrylic treatments were the least effective and silane-based treatments provided moderate reductions [1].
    • LT-DSC proved sensitive enough to detect chemically-driven distress mitigation benefits from sealers even when those same sealers did not improve other measured durability parameters [1].

    Novelty & rigor — The application of low-temperature differential scanning calorimetry to field-cored concrete powders provides a novel metric for assessing deicing-salt resistance by directly quantifying calcium oxychloride formation potential [2]. This quantitative approach establishes the first heat-release based link between sealer chemistry and mitigation of oxychloride risk, demonstrating unequivocally that commercial sealants reduce precipitation risk in ways not previously captured by other durability tests [1]. The method serves as a distinct screening tool that allows engineers to optimize cementitious blends for low-temperature environments and verify compliance with specific regulatory limits, capabilities that traditional durability assessments lack [3].

    Across the reviewed reports, LT-DSC consistently demonstrates a capacity to quantify calcium oxychloride levels and detect significant reductions in formation potential resulting from material modifications or protective treatments. Specific quantitative assessments reveal that modified binder mixes can lower calcium oxychloride content to approximately 8.4% compared to roughly 30.5% in conventional mixes, while various penetrating sealers also effectively reduce the potential for oxychloride formation relative to untreated controls. [1] [2]

    Implications — LT-DSC serves as a reliable diagnostic tool for quantifying calcium oxychloride formation, enabling the ranking of sealers and mix designs based on their ability to suppress expansive salt reactions [1]. The technique allows practitioners to identify chemical advantages in material formulations even when traditional durability metrics fail to show improvement [1].

    Limitations — Laboratory-derived quantification results have not yet been correlated with actual field performance, limiting the method’s reliability for durability prediction [2].
