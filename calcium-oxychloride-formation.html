    Calcium Oxychloride Formation

    chemical-reactionconcrete-deteriorationdeicing-saltsfly-ash-mitigation
    Also known as: MgCl2 damage, calcium oxychloride, calcium-magnesium oxychloride
    Created 2026-07-22 · Updated 2026-08-16T05:28:17Z

    Calcium oxychloride formation is a chemical deterioration process in concrete where chloride ions from deicing salts react with alkaline calcium compounds in hydrated cement paste to produce an expansive crystalline phase. This reaction typically occurs at low temperatures near freezing, resulting in the growth of platy crystals that occupy pore spaces and generate internal tensile stresses within the hardened matrix [2] [3]. The resulting expansion causes cracking and loss of structural integrity, particularly along joints or saw cuts, making it a recognized mechanism of chemical attack on cementitious materials [1] [3]. Because the expansive phase is stable only at low temperatures and breaks down at ordinary room temperatures, its presence indicates prior exposure to cold, chloride-rich environments [1].

    Key findings

    • Applying penetrating sealers markedly lowered the potential for calcium oxychloride formation by acting as a hydrophobic barrier that limited chloride ion ingress to hydrated cement [3].
    • Surface-densifier sealers provided the greatest reduction in calcium oxychloride formation, outperforming other chemistries such as acrylic barrier coatings which were the least effective [3].
    • Differential scanning calorimetry confirmed that every tested sealer decreased the risk of oxychloride reactions, indicating a consistent protective effect across different sealant types [3].
    • Laboratory measurements of calcium oxychloride formation potential were consistent with established literature, confirming expected behavior in concrete systems [1].
    • Once the calcium hydroxide content exceeded roughly one gram per hundred grams of powder, each additional gram of calcium hydroxide led to an increase in the amount of calcium oxychloride that could potentially form [1].

    Formation Mechanisms and Mitigation Strategies

    Calcium oxychloride forms when chloride ions from deicing salts react with alkaline calcium compounds in hydrated cement paste, producing an expansive crystalline phase [2] [3]. The growth of these platy crystals within pore spaces generates internal tensile stresses that lead to cracking and deterioration of the hardened concrete matrix [2] [3]. Mitigation strategies focus on limiting chloride ingress through surface treatments that create hydrophobic barriers, thereby reducing the reaction potential between hydrated cement and chloride ions at joint surfaces [3].

    Research problem — The formation of expansive calcium oxychloride during cold-weather exposure to chloride-based deicers causes premature cracking and joint deterioration, threatening the long-term durability of concrete pavements [2]. Mitigating this chemical distress requires effective strategies, such as incorporating high volumes of supplementary cementitious materials or applying hydrophobic sealers, to reduce the reaction potential between hydrated cement and chloride ions [3]. Accurate prediction of pavement performance is hindered by the difficulty in detecting these unstable phases and the complex relationship between calcium hydroxide content and the resulting expansive phase generation [1].

    State of the art — The industry has converged on low-temperature differential scanning calorimetry (LT-DSC) as the standard laboratory method for rapidly quantifying calcium oxychloride formation risk [1] [2] [3]. Mitigation strategies primarily rely on incorporating high-volume Class C fly ash or applying penetrating hydrophobic sealers to limit moisture and chloride ingress [2] [3].

    Findings from the reports

    • Modified M-QMC concrete with high fly ash dosage produced significantly less calcium oxychloride than conventional QMC mixes, resulting in markedly slower paste expansion [2].
    • All tested penetrating sealers reduced the amount of calcium oxychloride formed by acting as hydrophobic barriers that limited chloride ion ingress to hydrated cement [3].
    • Surface-densifier sealers provided the greatest reduction in calcium oxychloride formation, outperforming acrylic barrier coatings and silane-based products [3].
    • Microscopic examination revealed that treated concrete specimens contained far fewer large platy oxychloride crystals compared to untreated controls, corroborating quantitative reductions in formation [3].
    • Calcium hydroxide content above one gram per hundred grams of powder increased the potential calcium oxychloride formation by approximately two to four grams for each additional gram of calcium hydroxide [1].

    Novelty & rigor — The research introduces a distinctive approach by coupling low-temperature differential scanning calorimetry with thermodynamic modeling to directly quantify calcium oxychloride formation and predict its generation from mix-design parameters, offering a performance-based alternative to empirical testing [1]. This methodology provides the first systematic quantitative evidence that penetrating sealers can suppress calcium oxychloride formation by creating hydrophobic barriers that limit chloride ingress, establishing a direct link between sealer chemistry and durability mitigation [3]. Reproducibility is ensured through strict adherence to standardized low-temperature calorimetry protocols alongside detailed material selection and specimen preparation procedures that allow for faithful replication of the screening and assessment workflows [1] [2] [3].

    Mitigation strategies converge on limiting chloride access or altering the binder matrix, as both high-volume fly ash incorporation and the application of penetrating sealers significantly suppress oxychloride generation by restricting ion ingress and reducing reactive surface area. While chemical modifications like fly ash addition reduce oxychloride formation to a fraction of conventional mixtures, physical barriers such as lithium-salt or colloidal silica sealers provide the most substantial reduction in measured oxychloride mass by effectively blocking chloride penetration. [2] [3]

    Implications — Specifications should limit calcium hydroxide content by incorporating supplementary cementitious materials or reducing cement dosage to mitigate the increased vulnerability of high-CH mixes to expansive reactions [1]. Mix designs must include sufficient fly ash replacement and minimum cement content to substantially reduce oxychloride formation and prevent early joint cracking in cold-weather pavements [2]. Mitigation strategies should require suitable penetrating sealers, particularly surface densifiers, to restrict chloride ingress and curb the development of expansive crystals before microstructural densification occurs [3].

    Limitations — Laboratory findings regarding the reduction of calcium oxychloride formation have not yet been correlated with actual field performance, leaving a gap in validating these results under real-world conditions [2]. Observed reductions in formation potential may be influenced by limited exposure rather than solely the efficacy of sealers, as the testing environment did not actively promote oxychloride formation [3].
