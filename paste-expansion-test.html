    Paste Expansion Test

    concrete-durabilitydeicing-saltslaboratory-testtest-method
    Also known as: paste expansion measurement
    Created 2026-07-22 · Updated 2026-08-16T05:28:17Z

    The paste expansion test is a laboratory procedure designed to evaluate the susceptibility of concrete mixtures to damage caused by chemical reactions with deicing salts. The mechanism involves immersing thin slices of cured concrete in specific salt solutions, such as magnesium chloride, at low temperatures to simulate conditions that generate expansive compounds like calcium oxychloride within the paste matrix [1]. By measuring the resulting increase in thickness or the gap between aggregate and surrounding paste using precision tools, the test quantifies the material’s tendency to swell [1]. This measured expansion serves as an indicator of the concrete’s durability and its potential for salt-induced cracking over time [1].

    Sources (1)
    Evaluation of Modified Concrete Mixture Proportions for the City of West Des Moines (2016)

    Key findings

    • The paste-expansion test demonstrated that high-fly-ash M-QMC mixtures expanded significantly more slowly than conventional QMC and C4 mixes, which exhibited essentially identical expansion rates [1].
    • The study attributed the slower expansion of the M-QMC mix to fly ash content reducing calcium oxychloride formation [1].
    • Researchers observed a brief apparent increase in expansion for M-QMC specimens due to early-time surface exudate, an effect not present in the other mixes [1].

    Paste Expansion Test Results

    The test assesses concrete durability by measuring how much the cement paste swells when exposed to deicing salts that form expansive chemical compounds [1]. This expansion is quantified by immersing a thin slice of cured concrete in a magnesium chloride solution at low temperatures and recording the resulting increase in thickness or gap between aggregate and paste using precision gauges [1].

    Research problem — There is a need to understand how different concrete mix designs respond to deicing salts that can form expansive calcium oxychloride, as uncontrolled paste expansion leads to cracking and premature joint deterioration [1]. Research must determine whether modified mixes with high fly-ash content can effectively suppress oxychloride formation compared to conventional mixes when exposed to magnesium and calcium chloride deicers [1].

    State of the art — The paste-expansion test is an emerging field method for assessing concrete susceptibility to calcium oxychloride formation by measuring paste expansion around coarse aggregate after soaking cured specimens in a magnesium chloride solution [1]. Current practice involves taking initial expansion readings approximately 56 days after immersion and then every 28 days thereafter, with results demonstrating sensitivity to paste chemistry such as high fly-ash content [1].

    Findings from the reports

    • The high-fly-ash M-QMC mix expanded much more slowly than conventional QMC and C4 mixes, which exhibited essentially identical expansion rates [1].
    • Microscopic analysis revealed that the early rise in measured expansion for M-QMC specimens was caused by a removable surface exudate of Mg-rich compounds rather than true internal swelling, indicating limited bulk expansion [1].
    • The slower expansion observed in the high-fly-ash mix was attributed to its fly ash content reducing calcium oxychloride formation [1].

    Novelty & rigor — The study introduces a novel laboratory-based paste expansion test that isolates concrete slices to directly quantify oxychloride-induced swelling, combining quantitative gauge measurements with microstructural characterization to assess deicing-salt resistance [1]. This unique approach utilizes a standardized immersion protocol in chilled magnesium chloride solution and precise feeler gauge readings around coarse aggregate, providing a reproducible method for tracking paste expansion that is distinct from conventional field tests [1].

    Implications — The paste-expansion test serves as a practical indicator of deicing-salt resistance, supporting the recommendation to use high fly-ash dosages for improved durability in cold-weather pavements [1].

    Limitations — Surface exudates may have artificially influenced early expansion measurements, potentially skewing the initial data [1]. The correlation between laboratory-measured expansion and actual field performance remains unestablished and requires further investigation [1].
