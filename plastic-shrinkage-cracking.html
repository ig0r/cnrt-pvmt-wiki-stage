    Plastic Shrinkage Cracking

    concretecrackingearly-ageshrinkage
    Also known as: plastic-shrinkage
    Created 2026-07-22 · Updated 2026-08-17T15:56:38Z

    Plastic shrinkage cracking is a form of early-age distress that occurs in fresh concrete between casting and final set, while the material remains in its plastic phase. The phenomenon is driven by rapid surface moisture loss where evaporation exceeds the rate of bleed water replenishment, creating negative capillary pressures within the pore structure [1] [2]. These suction forces generate tensile stresses through differential contraction between the drying top layer and the interior, which can exceed the low early-age strength of the concrete [1] [2]. The resulting surface cracks are often amplified by external restraint from formwork or underlying elements that limit free shrinkage [2].

    Key findings

    • Inadequate curing and excessively high water-cement ratios produced fine surface cracks that opened the concrete to moisture entry, thereby accelerating deterioration mechanisms such as ASR and freeze-thaw damage [4].
    • Internal curing reduced the concrete’s ultimate shrinkage, which directly lowered the risk of early-age plastic shrinkage cracking and improved overall distress behavior over the service life [8].
    • Plastic shrinkage cracking emerged as a primary defect in 3D-printed concrete due to low water-to-binder ratios, but immediate covering with plastic sheets and high-humidity curing suppressed crack formation [11].
    • Substituting Portland cement with Class F fly ash improved the concrete’s resistance to plastic-shrinkage cracking while enhancing workability and durability [2].
    • Polypropylene microfibers at low dosages markedly reduced shrinkage-induced cracking by providing tensile strength and crack-bridging capabilities that practically eliminated plastic shrinkage cracks [2].
    • Adding Type K expansive cement narrowed crack widths and lowered tensile strain, but it also accelerated capillary-pressure development, which rendered it unsuitable for final bridge-deck mixes despite its crack-reducing benefits [2].

    Plastic Shrinkage Cracking Mechanisms and Mitigation Strategies

    Plastic shrinkage cracking is an early-age defect that occurs in fresh concrete between casting and final set, driven by rapid surface moisture loss that outpaces the upward migration of bleed water [1] [2]. This imbalance creates negative capillary pressures within the pore structure, generating tensile stresses that frequently exceed the very low early-age strength of the material [2]. The resulting surface cracks are exacerbated by external restraint from formwork or underlying elements, which restricts free shrinkage and amplifies the stress field [2]. Mitigation strategies primarily focus on maintaining adequate surface moisture to prevent excessive evaporation and limit early-age deformation before the concrete hardens [11].

    Research problem — Research must address how rapid surface moisture evaporation relative to internal bleeding generates tensile stresses that exceed the low early-age strength of concrete, leading to premature cracking before sufficient strength is attained [1] [9] [10]. Investigations are needed to understand how specific mix designs and construction practices, such as the use of retarding admixtures or highly absorptive aggregates, exacerbate plastic shrinkage risks in fresh concrete [5]. Studies must evaluate mitigation strategies, including internal curing techniques and prompt surface protection, to reduce early-age cracking and thereby preserve the durability and service life of pavement and structural elements [7] [8] [11].

    State of the art — Current practice for limiting plastic shrinkage cracking in high-performance concrete relies on internal curing, most commonly achieved by replacing a portion of fine aggregate with pre-wetted lightweight material to maintain internal relative humidity and suppress early-age shrinkage [7]. Mitigation strategies combine binder optimization using supplementary cementitious materials like fly ash with hybrid fiber reinforcement systems, such as polypropylene microfibres combined with alkali-resistant glass or high-aspect-ratio synthetic fibers, to enhance early-age tensile capacity [2]. In regions where superabsorbent polymers are utilized, they are classified as admixtures and dosed at low percentages of binder mass to replace evaporated water and reduce crack formation while maintaining durability [10].

    Findings from the reports

    • Internal curing reduced the concrete’s ultimate shrinkage, which lowered the risk of early-age plastic shrinkage cracking and allowed for thinner slabs or increased joint spacing without sacrificing performance [8].
    • Inadequate curing was identified as a primary cause of fine surface cracks that open the concrete to water entry, thereby accelerating deterioration mechanisms such as ASR, freeze-thaw damage, and ettringite formation [4].
    • Excessively high water-cement ratios produced pronounced cracking, particularly near construction joints, which provided pathways for moisture ingress [4].
    • Low water-to-binder ratios increased the propensity for plastic shrinkage cracking in 3D-printed concrete due to higher shrinkage potential required for buildability [11].
    • Immediate post-print curing, achieved by covering elements with a plastic sheet before transfer to standard conditions, markedly reduced both slump and plastic-shrinkage cracking [11].
    • Increasing the proportion of Type K expansive cement consistently reduced plastic-shrinkage crack width across various binder groups, including plain concrete, fly ash mixes, and silica-fume mixes [2].
    • Substituting part of the Portland cement with Class F fly ash improved the concrete’s resistance to plastic shrinkage cracking while enhancing workability and durability [2].
    • Polypropylene microfibers at low dosages markedly reduced shrinkage-induced cracking, although practical limits were recommended due to diminishing returns or handling issues [2].
    • Fiber reinforcement provided tensile strength and crack-bridging capabilities that could practically eliminate plastic shrinkage cracks in concrete mixes [2].

    Novelty & rigor — The research introduces a novel diagnostic framework for plastic-shrinkage cracking by utilizing sorptivity as a sensitive indicator of near-surface microstructure changes, thereby linking curing compound performance to pore structure and hydration levels rather than relying solely on moisture loss metrics [1]. A distinctive methodological contribution involves the adaptation of restrained-slab testing through the integration of capillary-pressure sensors and digital image correlation, which allows for the simultaneous monitoring of pore-water pressure and tensile strain fields to evaluate mix designs incorporating expansive cement and hybrid fibers [2]. The study uniquely identifies plastic-shrinkage cracking as a distinct defect in additive manufacturing, emphasizing that timely curing is critical for mitigating this specific failure mode during layer-by-layer deposition, separate from flow-related defects [11].

    Implications — Internal curing using saturated lightweight fine aggregate reduces early-age shrinkage and thermal expansion, enabling thinner pavements or wider joint spacing while lowering long-term maintenance costs [8]. Plastic shrinkage cracking serves as an entry point for water that accelerates durability issues such as alkali-silica reaction and freeze-thaw damage, making rigorous early-age moisture retention and mix workability control essential preventive measures [4]. For 3-D printed concrete, preventing shape instability requires immediate post-extrusion curing combined with mix designs that balance flowability with rapid stiffening to resist shrinkage stresses before the material gains sufficient strength [11]. Bridge deck specifications should exclude Type K expansive cement in favor of fly ash replacement for improved dimensional stability, and employ a hybrid fiber system combining polypropylene microfibers with alkali-resistant glass macrofibers to manage early-age cracking and residual strength [2].

    Limitations — Life-cycle cost analyses often exclude the direct financial benefits of reduced cracking and assume uniform maintenance costs, resulting in net present value estimates that likely understate the true economic advantages [8]. Printable concrete mixes are inherently vulnerable to plastic shrinkage due to low water-to-binder ratios required for buildability, making immediate and controlled curing essential to prevent slumping and cracking [11]. Certain mitigation agents like expansive cement can inadvertently increase capillary pressure, while the optimal dosage of such materials remains unverified due to limited testing ranges [2]. The practical application of microfibers is constrained by water demand and efficiency limits at higher dosages, and experimental validation of cracking predictions may be compromised by measurement gaps or lack of specimen failure during testing [2].
