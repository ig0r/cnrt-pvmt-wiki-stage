    Longitudinal Cracking

    crackingdistresspavement
    Also known as: Centerline Cracking, Longitudinal Cracks, Parallel Cracking
    Created 2026-05-20 · Updated 2026-08-17T00:30:08Z

    Longitudinal cracking is a pavement distress characterized by cracks that run parallel to the direction of traffic flow and longitudinal joints. In rigid concrete pavements, this failure mode occurs when tensile stresses within the slab exceed the material’s tensile strength, causing the concrete to pull apart along its length [7]. These internal tensile stresses arise from early-age volume changes due to temperature and moisture variations, thermal gradients that induce curling or warping, restraint from underlying layers, and wheel loads applied near joints or edges [1] [7]. The phenomenon also manifests as reflective cracking in overlays, where distress propagates upward from pre-existing longitudinal cracks in the underlying pavement structure rather than resulting directly from traffic loading on the new surface [14].

    Key findings

    • Longitudinal cracking prevalence increased with pavement age and lower Pavement Condition Index, while higher average daily traffic volumes further elevated crack frequency [1].
    • Early-age longitudinal cracking was primarily caused by mix design issues, material quality deficiencies, construction practices, heavy agricultural loads, and subgrade problems [1].
    • Extensive longitudinal cracks developed in the truck lane of a two-lift pavement soon after construction, originating directly from the wheel path [11].
    • Linking a widening joint directly to the concrete overlay prevented longitudinal cracking at that location, whereas conventional joint formers resulted in visible distress [10].
    • Longitudinal cracks tended to develop when the longitudinal joint was positioned close to or within the wheel path, indicating that precise joint placement is critical for controlling this distress [10].
    • In continuously reinforced concrete slabs, longitudinal cracking patterns were dictated by the position and spacing of the embedded reinforcement bars rather than new construction variables [6].
    • Longitudinal cracking persisted and worsened over time in bonded concrete overlays, particularly at locations where the original pavement had been repaired [6].
    • GPS-guided saws successfully aligned overlay joints within one inch of existing underlying joints, effectively controlling centerline cracking in bonded concrete pavements [15].

    Mechanisms and Mitigation of Longitudinal Cracking

    Longitudinal cracking in rigid concrete pavements is defined as distress where cracks run parallel to the direction of traffic, typically initiating when internal tensile stresses exceed the material’s tensile strength [1] [7]. These critical tensile stresses arise from early-age volume changes due to temperature and moisture variations, slab curling induced by thermal gradients, and restraint provided by the underlying base or adjacent joints [7]. Mitigation strategies primarily focus on controlling these stresses through proper joint design, including appropriate sawing timing and depth, adequate curing practices, and correct slab geometry to manage load distribution and frictional restraint [1] [7].

    Research problem — Research must determine why traditional fatigue models fail to predict longitudinal cracking in jointed plain concrete pavements, particularly when excessive tie-bars or wide joints are used [11]. Investigations are needed to understand how dowel bar binding and loss of load transfer efficiency shift flexural stresses to the joint region, thereby accelerating slab deterioration and initiating longitudinal cracks in wheel paths [5] [9]. Studies must evaluate how improper joint formation, such as delayed sawing or misalignment, and deep underlying joints interact with thin overlays to cause premature reflective cracking that undermines structural integrity [6] [13] [15]. Research is required to accurately locate and quantify longitudinal distresses during routine surveys, as surface observations often misrepresent internal damage severity, leading to inappropriate maintenance decisions [19].

    State of the art — Longitudinal cracking is currently understood as a multifactor distress driven by slab geometry, material properties, environmental loading, and construction practices such as joint sawing timing and subgrade friction [1] [7]. Mitigation strategies emphasize mechanistic design improvements, including the use of non-corrosive dowel materials, optimized slab aspect ratios, tied PCC shoulders, and precise longitudinal joint alignment using GPS-guided sawing or paver-mounted devices [7] [9] [15]. Detection and assessment have evolved from visual surveys to automated video analytics and nondestructive testing tools like ground-penetrating radar, while design frameworks are increasingly integrating mechanistic-empirical models and finite-element analysis to predict cracking behavior [3] [7] [19].

    Findings from the reports

    • Most observed longitudinal cracks initiated at transverse joints that separate the lane edge from the wheel path [7].
    • Numerical analysis demonstrated that skewed joint configurations raise tensile-stress ratios and increase cracking risk compared with rectangular joints [7].
    • Sections with tied Portland-cement-concrete shoulders exhibited virtually no longitudinal cracking, whereas granular shoulders suffered the highest frequencies and severities [7].
    • Increasing slab thickness above 11 inches eliminated observed longitudinal cracking in widened pavements [7].
    • Linking a widening joint directly to the concrete overlay completely prevented longitudinal cracking at that location, whereas sections using a conventional longitudinal joint former showed some cracking [10].
    • Longitudinal cracks tended to develop when the longitudinal joint was positioned close to or within the wheel path [10].
    • GPS-guided saws aligned overlay joints within about one inch of the existing joint, effectively controlling centerline cracking in bonded concrete pavements [15].
    • The redesigned slipform paver knife successfully formed a controlled longitudinal groove so cracks developed in a predetermined location [15].
    • Longitudinal cracking became more prevalent as concrete pavements aged and when the Pavement Condition Index was low [1].
    • The frequency of longitudinal cracks rose with higher average daily traffic volumes but lessened as the proportion of truck traffic increased [1].
    • Longitudinal cracking in continuously reinforced concrete slabs developed directly along the embedded reinforcement bars, dictated by their position and spacing [6].
    • Delayed joint sawing, slow early strength gain, and subbase stiffness contributed to longitudinal cracking in early-age concrete pavements [6].
    • Fiber reinforcement limited crack development by retaining tighter cracks and exhibiting lower overall cracking rates than plain-concrete sections in reflective cracking scenarios [14].
    • Longitudinal cracks in concrete overlays were primarily reflective, reproducing pre-existing longitudinal cracks in the asphalt base within the first year of service [14].
    • Sections with low joint load-transfer efficiency experienced notable surface distress including pronounced longitudinal and transverse cracks [8].
    • Longitudinal cracking accelerated the reduction in pavement condition index when associated with loss of support beneath the pavement due to erosion or inadequate drainage [16].
    • Field surveys and observations over five years showed no longitudinal cracks in the LMC-VE bridge deck overlay, indicating that this material system did not develop cracking parallel to the bridge span [18].

    Novelty & rigor — Research has shifted the focus of longitudinal cracking mitigation from post-construction repair to proactive design by integrating finite-element modeling with field surveys to identify critical geometric and loading factors, such as skewed joints and specific truck axle configurations, that drive stress concentrations [7]. Novel construction techniques have been developed to control cracking through the use of slip-form paver-mounted knives that create continuous weakened planes during placement, alongside GPS-guided sawing systems that ensure precise joint alignment and eliminate misalignment-induced distress [10] [15]. Distinctive analytical approaches now link longitudinal cracking directly to subgrade support degradation and altered drainage capacity by combining corrected falling-weight deflectometer data with in-situ permeability testing, while other studies quantify the mitigating effects of fiber reinforcement on reflective cracking from underlying asphalt layers [8] [14].

    Across the reviewed reports, longitudinal cracking mechanisms are primarily driven by structural deficiencies and construction geometry rather than uniform material properties, with studies identifying joint layout, shoulder support type, and slab thickness as critical design factors governing tensile stress distribution. While some investigations attribute cracking to early-age thermal stresses and delayed sawing in new concrete pavements, others find that crack patterns are strictly dictated by the position of embedded reinforcement or reflect pre-existing distresses from underlying asphalt layers. Mitigation strategies converge on precise joint alignment and robust shoulder support, as GPS-guided sawing and tied PCC shoulders significantly reduce cracking incidence compared to conventional manual methods or granular shoulders. Conversely, certain material systems such as LMC-VE overlays show no longitudinal cracking over extended service periods, suggesting that specific mix designs and shrinkage characteristics can effectively eliminate this distress mode entirely. [6] [7] [10] [14] [15] [18]

    Implications — Contractors should adopt precise joint placement methods, such as GPS-guided saws or specialized forming tools, to align overlay joints with existing pavement within tight tolerances and effectively control centerline cracking [15]. Designers must ensure well-compacted, engineered subgrades with adequate drainage and stability to mitigate cracking driven by poor foundation support and heavy loads, particularly as traffic volumes increase [1]. Two-lift concrete designs require strict control over material compatibility regarding shrinkage and stiffness differences, along with appropriate joint spacing and reinforcement, to prevent debonding-induced cracks at the layer interface [11]. Mitigation strategies must target reinforcement bar depth, spacing, and alignment, alongside revised joint construction practices such as notching lean concrete bases and re-configuring dowel-bar layouts for uniform performance [6]. Joint layout should utilize rectangular configurations with short spacings to minimize stresses, and tying widened slabs to Portland cement concrete shoulders is recommended over asphalt or granular options to significantly reduce crack frequency [7].

    Limitations — Validation of GPS-guided sawing and slipform knife designs remains preliminary, as current evidence is limited to single studies requiring broader field trials before widespread adoption [15]. Definitive identification of cracking causes is constrained by missing subgrade material data and a lack of longitudinal profile information over time [1]. Confidence in crack behavior conclusions is reduced because key historical pavement sections were removed, preventing re-testing of original reinforcement alignment effects [6]. The limited dataset and minimal investigation into skewed joints leave causal relationships between slab geometry and cracking ambiguous [7].

    Longitudinal Cracking in HMA Overlays

    Research problem — Longitudinal cracking outside the wheel path remains a dominant non-load-related distress in HMA overlays over rubblized PCC, often indicating inadequate slab breakage during construction [17]. The persistence of this distress despite increased overlay thickness suggests that current design and construction practices may not adequately address the underlying causes related to insufficient rubblization [17].

    Findings from the reports

    • Identified longitudinal cracking as a dominant non-load-associated distress on hot-mix-asphalt overlays placed over rubblized concrete, with observed crack counts ranging from two to fourteen across surveyed sites [17].
    • Attributed the occurrence of these longitudinal cracks primarily to insufficient breaking of the underlying concrete slabs or other construction-related factors rather than the rubblization process itself [17].
    • Reported that despite the prevalence of longitudinal cracking, the overall performance of rubblized sections remained very well with no fatigue or rutting problems observed [17].

    Novelty & rigor — The research introduces a systematic documentation approach that identifies longitudinal cracking as the primary non-load-related distress in HMA overlays over rubblized concrete, distinguishing it from fatigue or reflection mechanisms [17]. It establishes a reproducible validation framework for mechanistic-empirical design by correlating measured tensile and vertical strain limits with empirical durability thresholds to reliably predict overlay thickness requirements [17].

    Implications — Routine monitoring remains advisable to track these cracks, as their presence suggests the mechanistic-empirical design parameters are adequate for long-lasting performance [17].
