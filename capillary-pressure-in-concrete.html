    Capillary Pressure in Concrete

    concreteearly-agemoistureshrinkage
    Also known as: capillary pressure
    Created 2026-07-22 · Updated 2026-08-17T09:53:08Z

    Capillary pressure in concrete is the negative pore-water pressure, or suction, that develops within the cement paste matrix as water is removed through hydration, bleeding, or evaporation. This phenomenon arises when evaporation creates menisci within fine capillary pores, where surface tension pulls adjacent solid surfaces together to generate tensile stress in the hardened material [1]. The magnitude of this suction is determined by local pore characteristics such as air-entry time, while its rate of development is governed by the global drying conditions of the structural element [1]. When the concrete is restrained, these tensile stresses induced by capillary suction can exceed the material’s capacity, leading to early-age shrinkage cracking [1].

    Key findings

    • Increasing the proportion of Type K expansive cement accelerated early-age capillary pressure development, undermining resistance to plastic shrinkage [1].
    • Higher dosages of Type K expansive cement caused a pronounced rise in capillary pressure even when other supplementary cementitious materials were present [1].
    • Silica fume increased the water demand and consequently raised the rate of capillary pressure development [1].
    • Class F fly ash reduced the rate of capillary pressure development due to its spherical particles and lower water demand, making it the preferred supplementary cementitious material for controlling this phenomenon [1].

    Factors Influencing Capillary Pressure

    Capillary pressure arises from the negative pore-water suction generated when water is removed from cement paste through hydration, bleeding, or evaporation [1]. This phenomenon occurs as surface tension at menisci within fine capillary pores pulls solid surfaces together, creating tensile stress that drives matrix contraction [1]. The magnitude of the resulting pressure is determined by local pore characteristics such as air-entry time, while its rate of development depends on global drying conditions [1].

    Research problem — How does the accelerated development of capillary pressure during early-age concrete hardening contribute to plastic-shrinkage cracking in restrained structural elements [1]? What is the impact of specific cementitious materials, such as expansive cements, on the rate and magnitude of capillary pressure buildup and its subsequent effect on crack resistance [1]?

    State of the art — Current research identifies early-age capillary pressure as a primary driver of plastic shrinkage cracking, with prevailing practice utilizing embedded sensor systems to monitor pressure evolution and crack width during the first six hours after casting [1]. Mix-design strategies for controlling this pressure focus on binder chemistry, where silica fume increases the development rate due to higher water demand, while supplementary cementitious materials like Class F fly ash lower it by improving workability [1].

    Findings from the reports

    • Increased proportions of Type K expansive cement consistently accelerated the development of early-age capillary pressure in restrained slabs, regardless of the supplementary cementitious material type or dosage [1].
    • The addition of silica fume reduced the overall rate of capillary-pressure buildup despite its high water demand raising local suction levels [1].
    • Class F fly ash diminished the rate of capillary pressure development due to its spherical particle morphology and lower water demand requirements [1].
    • The accelerated capillary pressure induced by high dosages of Type K expansive cement undermined concrete resistance to plastic shrinkage, leading to recommendations against its use in final mix designs [1].
    • Class F fly ash was identified as the preferred supplementary cementitious material for controlling capillary pressure while maintaining other performance advantages [1].
    • The incorporation of super-absorbent polymers into concrete mixes reduced capillary suction compared to reference mixtures lacking these additives [2].

    Novelty & rigor — Novelty lies in treating the rate of capillary pressure development as a primary indicator for plastic shrinkage risk, rather than relying solely on static magnitude measurements [1]. The approach is distinct because it integrates continuous direct measurement with digital image correlation to map strain fields and quantify the mechanistic link between binder composition and pressure evolution [1]. A separate innovative contribution involves using superabsorbent polymers as internal curing agents, where dosage is systematically determined by hydration-based formulas to lower capillary suction [2].

    While silica fume is reported to increase the rate of capillary-pressure buildup due to high water demand, the addition of super-absorbent polymers consistently reduces overall capillary suction compared to reference mixtures lacking such additives. [1] [2]

    Implications — Designers should exclude Type K expansive cement from bridge-deck mixes because its acceleration of negative capillary pressure development compromises the concrete’s resistance to plastic shrinkage [1]. Specifications should incorporate Class F or Class C fly ash at moderate replacement levels to slow capillary pressure buildup and enhance durability, while balancing silica fume usage with adequate water-reducing admixtures to mitigate its tendency to increase pressure development rates [1]. Quality control practices should prioritize monitoring the rate of capillary pressure development as a global element property rather than focusing on absolute magnitude, using this metric to assess mix suitability and guide early-age crack mitigation strategies [1].

    Limitations — The use of Type K expansive cement accelerates capillary pressure development, which compromises resistance to plastic shrinkage and limits its suitability for bridge deck applications [1]. Sensor sensitivity to hidden air bubbles can cause premature gauge readings, meaning single-point measurements may not accurately represent slab-wide conditions without careful outlier filtering [1].
