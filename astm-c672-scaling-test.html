    ASTM C672 — Standard Test Method for Scaling Resistance of Concrete Surfaces Exposed to Deicing Chemicals

    deicerfreeze-thawscalingtest-method
    Also known as: ASTM C672, C672
    Created 2026-05-20 · Updated 2026-08-17T03:48:49Z

    This standardized laboratory procedure evaluates the resistance of horizontal concrete surfaces to scaling damage when exposed to deicing chemicals. The method subjects saturated concrete specimens to repeated freeze-thaw cycles while immersed in a saline solution, typically containing calcium chloride or sodium chloride [2] [3] [4]. Scaling resistance is quantified by measuring the mass loss of detached surface material after specific intervals of cycling [2] [3]. This approach provides a consistent mechanism for comparing how different concrete mixtures or protective treatments withstand the combined mechanical and chemical stresses of freezing, thawing, and chloride exposure [4].

    Key findings

    • Petrographic analysis revealed that construction-related factors such as retempering and high water-cement ratios were the primary drivers of scaling in field cores, rather than slag content [1].
    • The ASTM C672 test method proved overly aggressive for slag-containing concrete mixtures, yielding misleading results that did not correlate with good field performance observed for such concretes [2].
    • Compressive strength and rapid chloride permeability charge passed were identified as poor predictors of de-icer scaling resistance in the studied concrete mixtures [2].
    • Water-to-cement ratio, curing regime, air-void content, and slag replacement level dominated scaling performance more significantly than compressive strength or permeability metrics [2].
    • Alternative testing procedures, including the Quebec BNQ method and insulated one-dimensional freezing, produced results that were more consistent with field behavior than ASTM C672 for slag-containing mixes [2].
    • Most penetrating sealers worsened visual scaling ratings under ASTM C672 conditions compared to untreated concrete, except for 40% silane and lithium-salt densifiers which maintained control-level performance [4].
    • For concrete with an adequate air-void system, the application of surface sealers produced no appreciable difference in scaling resistance under ASTM C672 conditions [4].

    ASTM C672 Scaling Resistance Evaluation

    Scaling resistance evaluation assesses a concrete surface’s ability to withstand the combined mechanical stresses of freezing, thawing, and chemical exposure from deicing agents. The underlying mechanism involves repeated freeze-thaw cycles within a saline solution, which induces internal pressure and surface deterioration known as scaling or spalling [2] [3]. Quantitative assessment is achieved by measuring the mass loss of detached material from the specimen surface after specific intervals of cycling [2] [3].

    Research problem — Laboratory scaling tests based on ASTM C672 often produce results that contradict field performance for concrete containing supplementary cementitious materials, creating uncertainty in durability assessments [1] [2] [3]. The standard test method was originally developed for Portland cement only and may be excessively severe for modern mixes with slag or fly ash, potentially leading to the unnecessary rejection of sustainable mix designs [2] [3]. There is a need to determine whether surface treatments like penetrating sealers effectively mitigate scaling damage and how accurately standard visual ratings reflect actual material degradation under deicing conditions [4].

    State of the art — ASTM C672 remains the established standard laboratory procedure for evaluating deicer-induced scaling, utilizing specific specimen dimensions and freeze-thaw cycles with calcium chloride or sodium chloride solutions to quantify mass loss [1] [2]. The method is widely recognized as overly aggressive for modern mixes containing supplementary cementitious materials like slag, often yielding results that diverge from satisfactory long-term field performance [2]. Current industry consensus drives the development of modified protocols and draft replacements that incorporate controlled drying, insulated sides, and adjusted curing regimes to improve correlation with real-world conditions [2] [3].

    Findings from the reports

    • The study concluded that ASTM C672 is overly aggressive for slag-containing concrete mixtures and often yields misleading results that do not correlate with observed field performance [2] [3].
    • Field cores from most pavement and bridge-deck sites exhibited negligible scaling with mass losses well below regulatory limits, although one specific bridge deck showed significant deterioration linked to construction practices rather than material composition [1].
    • The ASTM C672 procedure consistently yielded lower scaling mass loss compared to alternative test methods such as BNQ and VDOT procedures, indicating it is less aggressive in measuring surface deterioration [2].
    • Reducing the water-to-cement ratio produced the most pronounced improvement in scaling resistance, allowing all tested mixtures to meet strict scaling criteria under the ASTM C672 protocol [2].
    • Compressive strength and rapid chloride permeability tests were found to be poor predictors of de-icer scaling resistance under the ASTM C672 method [2].
    • Most penetrating sealers worsened visual scaling ratings compared to untreated controls under ASTM C672 evaluation, with only specific silane and lithium-salt treatments maintaining performance comparable to the control [4].
    • Concrete specimens treated with 40% silane or SoTa sealers maintained high relative dynamic modulus values through extended freeze-thaw cycles, indicating strong resistance to deicing-salt scaling per ASTM C672 [4].
    • For concrete possessing an adequate air-void system, the application of sealers produced no appreciable difference in scaling performance, suggesting that proper air entrainment dominates resistance under ASTM C672 conditions [4].

    Novelty & rigor — The study introduced the use of core specimens harvested from in‑service slag‑cement pavements and bridge decks within the scaling‑resistance evaluation, creating a direct laboratory–field correlation that isolates construction variables [1]. It also presented an alternative protocol that changes solution chemistry, adds a pre‑saturation soak, and insulates specimen sides to enforce single‑directional freezing, thereby delivering a more representative assessment for slag‑rich concretes [2]. Inter‑laboratory testing of this new method demonstrated consistent results across independent facilities, confirming its reproducibility and supporting its consideration as a robust replacement for the conventional approach [3].

    Across the reviewed reports, ASTM C672 scaling resistance evaluations consistently demonstrate that lower water-to-cement ratios and adequate air entrainment are critical for minimizing mass loss, with specimens meeting these criteria generally exhibiting negligible deterioration under standard test conditions. However, the application of ASTM C672 to concrete containing supplementary cementitious materials like slag often yields scaling results that are more severe than those observed in field performance, suggesting the method may be overly aggressive for such mixtures. Furthermore, while surface sealers can influence dynamic modulus retention during freeze-thaw cycling, many penetrating treatments tend to exacerbate visual scaling ratings compared to untreated controls unless the concrete possesses an adequate air-void system that dominates resistance. [1] [2] [3] [4]

    Implications — Agencies should recognize that ASTM C672 is overly aggressive for slag-containing mixes, leading to unjustified restrictions on supplementary cementitious materials and necessitating a shift toward more field-representative testing methods [2] [3]. Because the test outcomes are driven primarily by mix proportions and construction quality rather than material composition alone, practitioners must prioritize rigorous air-void system design and proper curing to ensure durability [1] [2]. The standard remains the definitive evaluation tool for assessing how surface treatments affect scaling resistance, requiring that only sealers demonstrated not to degrade performance be specified for deicing environments [4].

    Limitations — The test method was originally developed for Portland cement concretes, making it potentially overly aggressive and misleading when applied to modern mixes containing supplementary cementitious materials [2]. Laboratory conditions often exaggerate scaling severity compared to typical field environments due to factors such as accelerated cooling rates, continuous fluid contact, and the absence of oxychloride salt formation [3] [4]. The standard may fail to accurately capture the protective performance of surface treatments like penetrating sealers and lacks sufficient correlation with actual field service outcomes [2] [4].
