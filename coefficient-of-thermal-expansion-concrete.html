    Coefficient of Thermal Expansion (Concrete)

    concrete-propertiesmaterial-characterizationthermal
    Also known as: CTE, coefficient of thermal expansion, thermal expansion coefficient
    Created 2026-05-20 · Updated 2026-08-17T09:53:08Z

    The coefficient of thermal expansion for concrete is a material property that quantifies the fractional change in length per unit change in temperature. This behavior arises from the combined thermal responses of its primary constituents, specifically the cement paste and the mineral aggregates [2] [3]. Because the aggregate phase typically exhibits a lower expansion coefficient than the surrounding cement matrix, the overall value is predominantly governed by the type, proportion, and thermal characteristics of the aggregates used in the mix [2] [3]. The specific magnitude of expansion is further modulated by mix design variables such as the water-to-cement ratio, cement type, and the moisture condition or age of the concrete [3].

    Key findings

    • Limestone-based concrete mixes exhibited significantly lower CTE values compared to quartzite, dolomite, and gravel-containing mixes, confirming that limestone is the preferred aggregate for minimizing thermal distortion in jointed plain concrete pavements [2] [3].
    • High-coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) aggregates, such as quartz and gravel, produced roughly twice the slab curvature of lower-CTE limestone aggregates, establishing aggregate type as a primary driver of early-age curling [2].
    • Measured CTE values for various coarse aggregates exceeded the default MEPDG design value, demonstrating that generic default inputs are insufficient and aggregate-specific measurements are required for accurate pavement performance prediction [3].
    • Internally cured concrete exhibited a lower coefficient of thermal expansion compared to conventionally cured concrete, which helped offset performance penalties associated with wider joint spacing and improved overall distress resistance [6].

    Aggregate Mineralogy and Internal Curing Effects on Concrete CTE

    The coefficient of thermal expansion quantifies the dimensional change of a material per unit temperature variation, with concrete’s overall value determined by the relative proportions and properties of its cement paste and aggregate constituents [3]. The specific mineralogy of the aggregate plays a critical role in this behavior, as different rock types possess distinct intrinsic expansion rates that directly influence the concrete’s susceptibility to thermal strains and subsequent deformation [2].

    Research problem — Conventional concrete exhibits a relatively high coefficient of thermal expansion, which generates significant temperature-induced stresses that lead to curling, warping, joint faulting, and premature cracking in pavements [4] [6] [7] [9] [10]. There is a critical need for reliable coefficient of thermal expansion data specific to local aggregate mineralogy, as default design values may not accurately reflect the behavior of regional materials [3]. Current predictive models and finite-element tools often fail to adequately incorporate hardened concrete properties like thermal expansion, limiting the ability to forecast thermally induced stresses and optimize mix designs such as internal curing for improved durability [2] [5].

    State of the art — Current practice determines concrete’s coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) primarily through standardized laboratory testing per AASHTO TP-60, supplemented by field-based strain measurements to capture directional and anisotropic variations [2]. Aggregate mineralogy is recognized as the dominant factor influencing CTE values, with quartzite yielding higher expansion than dolomite or limestone, necessitating aggregate-specific measured or calibrated inputs for accurate mechanistic-empirical design rather than relying on generic defaults [3]. Internal curing using pre-saturated lightweight fine aggregate is established as a method to lower concrete CTE and thermal strain, offering benefits such as reduced cracking potential that can enable thinner pavement sections or wider joint spacing [6].

    Findings from the reports

    • Internally cured concrete exhibited a lower coefficient of thermal expansion than conventionally cured concrete, which helped offset performance penalties associated with wider joint spacing and improved overall pavement distress behavior [6].
    • The type of coarse aggregate strongly controlled the concrete’s coefficient of thermal expansion, with limestone-based mixes showing the lowest values and quartzite mixes producing the highest values [3].
    • High-coefficient of thermal expansion aggregates such as quartz and gravel produced roughly twice the curvature of lower-coefficient limestone aggregates, making limestone the preferred aggregate for limiting permanent curl and warp in jointed plain concrete pavement [2].
    • The measured coefficient of thermal expansion values for tested samples exceeded the default MEPDG value, indicating that design guides should utilize aggregate-specific inputs rather than a single generic value [3].
    • Coefficient of thermal expansion measurements demonstrated modest reductions with age as the concrete cured, although values remained within expected ranges and satisfied construction specifications [4].

    Novelty & rigor — The research introduces a novel approach by quantifying how replacing normal-weight fine aggregate with pre-saturated lightweight material creates internal water reservoirs that reduce the concrete’s coefficient of thermal expansion, thereby mitigating thermal stress impacts [6]. This method is distinct because it utilizes internally cured concrete mixes to achieve uniformly distributed moisture, offering a unique mechanism for lowering thermal expansion compared to conventional curing practices [6].

    Across the reviewed reports, aggregate mineralogy is established as a primary determinant of concrete’s coefficient of thermal expansion, with limestone-based mixes consistently exhibiting lower CTE values compared to higher-expansion aggregates such as quartzite and gravel. While internal curing further reduces the coefficient of thermal expansion relative to conventional curing methods, this effect is distinct from the mineralogical control exerted by aggregate type, which remains the dominant factor in determining baseline thermal expansion properties. [1] [2] [3] [6]

    Implications — Designers should specify low-CTE aggregates, such as limestone, to minimize early-age curling and warping risks associated with high-expansion materials like quartz or gravel [2]. Pavement design guides must replace universal default values with aggregate-specific CTE inputs to ensure accurate joint spacing and reinforcement calculations [3]. The adoption of internally cured concrete allows for reduced thermal stresses, enabling designers to optimize slab thickness or increase joint spacing while maintaining long-term serviceability [6].

    Limitations — The limited sample size prevents thorough calibration of prediction equations and restricts confidence in recommended values for aggregates other than limestone [3]. Observed thermal-expansion-related behaviors are preliminary and site-specific due to the small amount of field data available [2]. Potential specimen variations and equipment calibration errors introduce uncertainty into the exact coefficient of thermal expansion measurements [4].

    Findings from the reports

    • The report included a field for the concrete’s coefficient of thermal expansion in both bonded-slab and unbonded-slab design tables, but left the entry blank, indicating that the specific coefficient was not provided [10].

    Novelty & rigor — Existing literature lacks a dedicated step-by-step protocol for measuring concrete thermal expansion, often relegating it to secondary status within broader fatigue or material modeling studies [5]. Reproducibility is hindered by the absence of specific details regarding mix designs, specimen dimensions, instrumentation calibration, and data analysis standards in these general frameworks [5].
