    Photogrammetry for Pavement Measurement

    cameraconceptmeasurementndtpavement
    Also known as: pavement photogrammetry
    Created 2026-05-20 · Updated 2026-08-17T17:47:38Z

    Photogrammetry for pavement measurement is a non-contact surveying technique that derives three-dimensional surface information from two-dimensional images captured by cameras positioned at known locations. The method relies on acquiring overlapping photographs to identify common points on the pavement, which are then used to compute spatial coordinates through triangulation [1]. This process yields elevation data that describe road profile, cracks, joints, and texture [1]. The technique may employ traditional film or modern digital sensors across various spectral bands to improve feature detection [1]. Accuracy depends on camera optics quality, imaging height above the pavement, availability of ground-control points, and precise knowledge of the camera’s position and orientation [1].

    Key findings

    • The study found that photogrammetry could provide relative elevation data for high‑speed pavement deflection measurement only when a camera array captured the entire deflection basin simultaneously at very high frame rates; otherwise absolute positioning errors prevented precise absolute measurements [1].
    • The report demonstrated that high‑speed digital cameras with thousands of frames per second and millimetre‑scale pixel resolution were available, enabling photogrammetry to map deflection‑basin shape and joint load transfer in a time‑coincident manner, provided orientation and ground‑control accuracy could be managed [1].
    • The authors reported that manual point matching was impractical for the required dense point clouds and that existing automated matching introduced excessive errors, limiting the feasibility of precise absolute measurements [1].
    • The investigation measured joint longitudinal transverse eccentricity (LTE) with sufficient accuracy using high‑speed digital cameras, detecting fault movements on the order of ten mils, indicating LTE as the most readily assessable pavement condition photogrammetrically [1].
    • The study observed that reliable mounting and operation of the camera array remained challenging despite the measurement capability [1].
    • The analysis concluded that orientation and ground‑control accuracy needed to be carefully managed to achieve accurate elevation measurements with photogrammetry [1].

    High-Speed Photogrammetry for Deflection Measurement

    Photogrammetry is a non-contact surveying technique that derives three-dimensional surface information from two-dimensional images captured by cameras positioned at known locations [1]. By acquiring overlapping photographs, common points on the pavement can be identified and their spatial coordinates computed through triangulation to yield elevation data describing road profile and texture [1].

    Research problem — High-speed photogrammetry on rigid pavements is fundamentally limited by the inability to achieve the sub-mil image orientation and ground-control accuracy required for absolute deflection or joint-height measurements when the sensor is mounted on a moving vehicle [1]. Significant practical difficulties exist in mounting and operating cameras reliably enough to capture very small pavement movements, such as deflection and faulting, while vehicles travel at highway speeds [1]. The lack of objective measurements due to these technical limitations leaves engineers without the proper information needed to make accurate maintenance or overlay decisions, risking costly misjudgments [1].

    State of the art — Current practice restricts high-speed photogrammetry to relative measurements of deflection basins using multiple cameras, as automated absolute elevation methods remain too error-prone for routine use [1]. While high-speed digital systems can capture sub-millimeter joint movements at rates up to 1000 frames per second, significant practical hurdles in vehicle-borne orientation control and data processing prevent the technology from becoming a fully automated tool for absolute pavement profiling [1].

    Findings from the reports

    • High-speed photogrammetry captured concrete joint deflection with pixel resolution on the order of one mil and detected movement as small as ten mils [1].
    • The study demonstrated that high-speed cameras are available with the speed and millimeter-scale resolution required for pavement deflection measurements [1].
    • The report showed that photogrammetry can provide relative elevation data for measuring pavement deflection basins when a camera array captures the entire basin simultaneously at very high frame rates [1].
    • The analysis revealed that absolute positioning errors preclude precise absolute measurements unless cameras are configured to photograph the entire or most of the deflection basin at one time [1].
    • The evaluation found that manual point matching is impractical for generating the required dense point clouds, while current automated matching introduces excessive errors [1].
    • The findings indicated that joint load transfer efficiency (LTE) can be measured with sufficient accuracy using high-speed digital cameras, making it the easiest pavement condition to assess via this method [1].

    Novelty & rigor — This work introduces the first field demonstration of using high-speed, high-resolution cameras to capture sub-millimeter pavement movements at vehicle speeds [1]. The approach is distinctive for applying ultra-high-speed photogrammetry with time-coincident measurements to enable real-time structural evaluation during highway travel, a capability not supported by traditional low-frame-rate imaging methods [1]. Reproducibility relies on mounting high-speed cameras at specific frame rates and shutter speeds to record joint surface movements under varying vehicle loads and speeds, though stable field deployment remains a practical challenge [1].

    Implications — Practitioners should recognize that high-speed photogrammetry is limited to relative elevation measurements rather than absolute surveys due to vehicle motion constraints [1]. Systemwide rigid-pavement management efforts should prioritize the assessment of joint longitudinal texture and elevation as the most feasible application for this technology [1]. Future deployment of these systems requires refined sensor mounting, advanced processing workflows, and potentially multispectral enhancements to maintain accuracy within current technical limits [1].

    Limitations — The manual identification of points is impractical due to the large volume of elevations required, while current automated matching methods produce excessive errors [1]. Achieving the necessary accuracy requires image orientation parameters that are too precise for a moving vehicle, and typical cameras lack sufficient frame rate and resolution [1]. Reliable field operation of high-speed cameras is difficult, and the overall concept operates at the edge of current precision limits, requiring further refinement before routine use [1].
